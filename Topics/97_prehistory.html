A. How Prehistory Is Periodised

Prehistory is divided by the material tools were made from. In 1836 the Dane C.J. Thomsen sorted the Copenhagen collections into a Stone, Bronze and Iron Age — the parent scheme; in 1865 John Lubbock split the Stone Age into Palaeolithic and Neolithic, and the Mesolithic was inserted later. V. Gordon Childe added the Neolithic Revolution and the Urban Revolution. Geologically the Palaeolithic is Ice-Age Pleistocene; after c. 10,000 BCE it is Holocene.

B. The Palaeolithic — Lower, Middle, Upper

Subdivided by technique, not date: Lower Palaeolithic people shaped the core, Middle Palaeolithic people worked the flakes struck off it, Upper Palaeolithic people made blades and used bone.

PhaseApprox. dateTechnologyType-sites
Lowerc. 2 million–100,000 BPCore tools — hand-axes, cleavers, choppers (Acheulian)Attirampakkam, Pallavaram (TN); Hunsgi–Isampur (Karnataka); Didwana; Soan valley
Middlec. 100,000–40,000 BPFlake tools — scrapers, points, borers; Levallois prepared-core methodNevasa on the Pravara ("Nevasian"); Narmada and Son valleys; Bhimbetka
Upperc. 40,000–10,000 BPBlades, burins, bone tools; art and ornament appearBelan valley and Lohanda Nala (UP); Baghor I (MP); Kurnool caves (AP); Patne
  • Two Lower Palaeolithic traditions: the Soan pebble-tool tradition of the north-west (de Terra and Paterson, 1935) and the Madrasian hand-axe tradition of the south.
  • Attirampakkam (TN) is the key site: Acheulian tools dated to about 1.5 million years, with Middle Palaeolithic-type tools by about 385,000 years — far earlier than older textbooks allow.
  • Narmada Man: a partial skull-cap from Hathnora, Sehore district, MP, found by Arun Sonakia in 1982 — the only pre-modern hominin fossil from India; first called Homo erectus narmadensis, now read as archaic Homo sapiens.
  • Earliest ritual and ornament: Baghor I yielded a stone platform holding a coloured triangular stone, widely read as a shrine; a bone figurine from Lohanda Nala is called a mother goddess; engraved ostrich eggshell beads come from Patne.
  • Where sites are absent: the deep alluvium of the Indus and Ganga plains, which has no stone outcrops. Sites cluster on the Deccan plateau, Vindhyas and Chotanagpur.

C. Robert Bruce Foote — the Start of Indian Prehistoric Archaeology

  • A geologist of the Geological Survey of India, the "Father of Indian Prehistory." On 30 May 1863 he found a Palaeolithic hand-axe at Pallavaram near Madras — the first recognised Stone Age artefact from India; with William King he identified Attirampakkam the same year.
  • Why it mattered: it proved human antiquity in India ran far deeper than the literary tradition suggested, turning prehistory into a science. He also opened up the southern Neolithic at Sanganakallu.
Bhimbetka, Raisen district, MP — UNESCO 2003. Trap: it lies near Bhopal but in Raisen district, and although the shelters hold Lower Palaeolithic deposits the paintings are overwhelmingly Mesolithic and later.

D. The Mesolithic — Microliths and the First Domesticates

The Ice Age ended around 10,000 BCE; big herd animals thinned and smaller game, fish and plant food took over. The answer was the microlith — a 1–5 cm tool of chert, jasper, agate or chalcedony, hafted in rows onto wood or bone as a composite tool: the first technology built from standard interchangeable parts.

  • Two families: non-geometric (blades, points, burins) and geometric (triangles, trapezes, lunates), the latter marking the later Mesolithic; Langhnaj (Gujarat) and Birbhanpur (West Bengal) extend the map west and east.
  • Domestication begins before the Neolithic — cattle, sheep and goat at Adamgarh (MP) and Bagor (Rajasthan), so "Mesolithic people were purely hunter-gatherers" is false.
  • First cemeteries and first pots: Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha and Damdama (eastern UP) gave dozens of skeletons, grave goods and a rib with a microlith embedded in it; India's earliest handmade pottery comes from the proto-Neolithic level at Chopani Mando, so pottery need not wait for agriculture.
  • Why it matters: the Mesolithic is the hinge — camps, burial, art and tame animals all appear here, and the Neolithic merely adds cultivated plants.

E. Bhimbetka and Prehistoric Rock Art

  • Basics: Vindhyan sandstone hills, Raisen district, MP; found by V.S. Wakankar, 1957; about 750 shelters, roughly 500 painted; UNESCO site, 2003. Pigments are all mineral — red haematite (geru), white limestone or kaolin, green from a copper-bearing stone, yellow ochre — which is why they survived in the porous rock.
  • What is depicted: earliest figures are large linear animals (bison, elephant, rhinoceros); the rich Mesolithic layer shows small hunting scenes — archers, animal drives, communal dancing, honey collection, mother-and-child groups; later layers add horse riders, swords and battle scenes, plainly historic.
  • Why it matters: rock art is the only direct window on prehistoric social life — group hunting and dancing prove cooperation and ritual, and the absence of crop scenes is itself evidence of a foraging economy.
PYQ ALERT What BPSC actually asks on sites: almost always site–state or site–district matching. Recurring traps: Chirand shown as Rohtas (correct: Saran) and Senuwar shown as Saran (correct: Rohtas) — the Bihar pair is deliberately swapped; Bhimbetka as Bhopal (correct: Raisen); Burzahom in Ladakh (correct: Kashmir); Mehrgarh in Sindh (correct: Balochistan); Bagor in MP (correct: Rajasthan); Inamgaon in Gujarat (correct: Maharashtra). Learn the district, not just the state.

F. The Neolithic Revolution

The Neolithic is defined by food production, not by polished stone. Its tool marker is the celt, a ground-and-polished axe strong enough to fell trees; its social marker is the permanent village.

  • Mehrgarh (Bolan pass, Balochistan), dug from 1974 by J.-F. Jarrige, is the earliest farming village in South Asia, c. 7000 BCE. Its first phase is aceramic — farming before pottery. Evidence: wheat, barley, domesticated zebu, sheep, goat, mud-brick houses, granaries, and the world's earliest cotton.
  • Its exam significance: it gives the Indus civilisation a local ancestry — Harappan cities need no migration to explain them.
  • Crops by region: wheat and barley in the north-west and Kashmir; rice in the Middle Ganga and Vindhyan zone; ragi and horse gram in the south. Rice is the eastern signature — which is exactly why Chirand matters.
  • Consequences: surplus → population growth → storage → heritable property → craft specialisation → ranking → eventually the city. The cost: early farmers were shorter and more disease-ridden than foragers — farming bought numbers, not comfort.

H. Chalcolithic Cultures — Copper Enters the Village

Farming villages now used copper alongside stone blades. These were rural, non-urban, non-literate cultures, many contemporary with the Harappans and several outliving them. Their shared marker is Black-and-Red Ware — black inside and at the rim, red outside, from inverted firing. In the Deccan they carried on without iron until about 700 BCE.

  • Daimabad, the largest Jorwe site, gave the bronze hoard — chariot with driver, buffalo, elephant, rhinoceros. Inamgaon (Pune) preserves a whole village: about 130 houses, a five-roomed chief's house, granaries, and an embankment and channel — India's earliest Chalcolithic water works.
  • Burials: adults were buried north–south under the house floor, sometimes with the feet cut off (R.S. Sharma reads this as fear of the dead returning); children went into twin urns joined mouth to mouth. The many child burials indicate high infant mortality.

I. Megalithic Burials of South India

Across peninsular India, roughly 1200–300 BCE, the dead were buried under or inside constructions of large stones. Megaliths are the Iron Age of the south, yielding iron, Black-and-Red Ware, carnelian beads and horse equipment. Wheeler's 1947 dig at Brahmagiri (Chitradurga) fixed the sequence Neolithic → Megalithic → early historic.

  • Why they matter: iron technology, bead trade, the horse and organised communal labour appear in the south before any state existed there, and the iron-and-BRW levels sit directly beneath the earliest Sangam-age levels.
  • Inequality made visible: some burials hold dozens of iron weapons and hundreds of beads, others almost nothing. Trap: megaliths are not exclusively southern — Vidarbha and the north-east have them too.

MethodWhat it datesRange / note

  • Best trap here: radiocarbon cannot date the Lower Palaeolithic — two million years is far beyond its range, so any option pairing C-14 with hand-axe dating is wrong.
  • "BP" means before present, with "present" fixed at 1950 — the year nuclear testing began altering the carbon record. Dates keep moving as techniques improve, so learn the sequence before the numbers.
The sequence in one frame: core tools → flake tools → blades and burins → microliths → polished celt. Trap: polishing, not size, defines a Neolithic tool, and microliths are Mesolithic — a small tool is not automatically the latest one.

K. What Settled Agriculture Changed Socially

  • Property and specialisation: a field and a granary cannot be carried away, so fixed assets create heritable property and land disputes; the granaries at Mehrgarh and storage jars at Inamgaon mean some people could eat without producing food — the precondition for priests, chiefs and metalworkers.
  • Ranking and ritual: the five-roomed house at Inamgaon, unequal megalithic grave goods and the Balathal wall imply leaders and directed labour; burial beneath house floors and mother-goddess figurines show a religion of fertility and ancestors. What did not change: no writing, weights, drainage or towns — that leap belongs to the Harappans (Topic 98).

(4) "Which is NOT a Palaeolithic feature?" — always the farming, pottery or village option. N. The Iron Age Bridge — Prehistory into History

  • Dates: iron is present in India by roughly 1200 BCE, with earlier dates claimed from Malhar and Raja Nala-ka-tila (UP); in the south it appears at Hallur around 1000 BCE.
  • Why it mattered: the iron ploughshare and axe cleared the forested soils of the Middle Ganga plain that copper could not touch; the rice surplus that followed produced taxable villages, territorial states, the sixteen Mahajanapadas, Magadha's rise, and the second urbanisation (c. 600 BCE) at Pataliputra, Rajgir, Vaishali and Champa.
🧠 Mnemonic 1 — the sequence "P-M-N-C-I":
Palaeolithic (hunt) → Mesolithic (microliths) → Neolithic (farm) → Chalcolithic (copper) → Iron (plough). "Please Make No Careless Idiocy." Underneath sits the tool logic: core → flake → blade → microlith → celt → metal.
🧠 Mnemonic 2 — Chalcolithic cultures oldest to youngest, "A-K-M-J":
Ahar (c. 2600 BCE) → Kayatha (c. 2000) → Malwa (c. 1700) → Jorwe (c. 1400). "Aaj Kal Mera Jeevan." The belt drifts south-east and later in time, Banas to Godavari.
🧠 Mnemonic 3 — southern Neolithic ash-mound belt:
Brahmagiri, Maski, Piklihal, Sanganakallu, Hallur, Tekkalakota — "Big Monkeys Play Silly Hide-and-seek Together." All Karnataka, all cattle-and-millet; Hallur adds the earliest iron in the south.
🔶 Bihar Connection
Why Bihar's prehistory looks the way it does

The Bihar plain is deep Ganga alluvium with no stone outcrops, so Palaeolithic tool-makers had no raw material and left almost no trace on the plain itself. Bihar's Stone Age evidence sits on the southern hill fringe — the Kharagpur hills of Munger, the Gaya uplands, the Kaimur–Rohtas plateau. The plain becomes visible only from the Neolithic, when rice farmers settled the river banks.

Chirand (Saran district) — the site BPSC returns to
  • Where exactly: the northern bank of the Ganga near Chhapra, close to the Ganga–Ghaghra confluence — water, fish and fertile silt, which is why the mound was never abandoned.
  • What makes it special: an exceptional bone and antler industry — arrowheads, points, needles, borers, combs, ornaments — richer than at almost any other Indian Neolithic site. The floodplain had antler in plenty but poor stone, so the technology adapted. If asked which Indian Neolithic site is famous for bone tools, the answer is Chirand.
  • Economy and material culture: rice with wheat, barley, lentil and moong; cattle, sheep, goat and pig; hunting and fishing continuing alongside farming; celts and microliths; red, black, Black-and-Red and corded ware; terracotta figurines; stone beads; circular wattle-and-daub huts.
  • Its real importance: a continuous stratified sequence from Neolithic through Chalcolithic to NBPW/Iron Age — one mound recording the journey from the first rice village to the Mahajanapadas.

P. Bihar Points Worth Memorising

  • District pairs are the trap: Chirand = Saran, Senuwar = Rohtas, Taradih = Gaya, Paisra = Munger — Chirand and Senuwar are swapped more often than any other pair.
  • Bihar is a rice Neolithic, not a wheat Neolithic. Mehrgarh and Burzahom grew wheat and barley; Chirand and Senuwar grew rice — placing Bihar in the eastern/Middle Ganga zone with Koldihwa, Mahagara and Lahuradewa.
  • Bihar's Neolithic is late, not early — late third to early second millennium BCE, millennia after Mehrgarh. The lateness is environmental: dense forest and flood-prone land needed heavier tools, which is why iron later transformed the region so completely.
  • Rock shelters with prehistoric paintings are reported from the Kaimur range in Kaimur and Rohtas districts — the same sandstone belt that carries Bhimbetka further west, and Bihar's least studied rock-art zone.
  • Bihar has no Harappan sites. The Indus civilisation never reached the Middle Ganga plain; Bihar went from Neolithic to Chalcolithic villages to iron with no urban Bronze Age, so its first cities are second-urbanisation ones.
🔗 Cross-Topic Connections
  • Topic 98 — Indus Valley Civilisation: Mehrgarh supplies the Neolithic ancestry of the Harappans, so the Indus cities are the local outcome of the farming revolution described here. The Harappans are India's Bronze Age; the Chalcolithic villages were their rural contemporaries, and Ahar and Jorwe outlived them.
  • Topic 99 — Vedic Age: the archaeological correlate of the later Vedic period is Painted Grey Ware plus iron (c. 1200–600 BCE) at Hastinapur, Atranjikhera and Ahichchhatra. The eastward Vedic movement into the doab is the same expansion iron enabled — text and trowel on one process.
  • Topic 100 — Mahajanapadas: iron ploughshare → forest clearance → rice surplus → taxable villages → territorial states → sixteen Mahajanapadas and the second urbanisation. NBPW is the shared marker of that horizon — at Pataliputra and Rajgir, and atop the Chirand mound.
  • Topic 106 — Ancient Art & Literature: Bhimbetka begins every Indian art-history chain — Bhimbetka → Harappan seals → Mauryan pillar capitals → Ajanta. Prehistoric art is anonymous and animal-centred; historic art is commissioned, religious and human-centred.
  • Topic 155 — Ancient Bihar: Chirand, Senuwar, Taradih, Sonpur and Maner are the pre-chapter to Ancient Bihar, establishing continuous settlement of the plain from the Neolithic onwards — the manpower and food base Magadha mobilised to build India's first empire.
📰 Current Relevance
  • Bhimbetka: UNESCO World Heritage Site (2003) — one of the most important prehistoric art sites in the world. Attracts researchers and tourists from around the world.
  • Mehrgarh: now in Pakistan (Balochistan) — but culturally part of the Indian subcontinent's prehistoric heritage. It shows the earliest evidence of farming in South Asia.
  • Neolithic Revolution: the transition from hunting-gathering to farming is still studied as the most important revolution in human history — it led to settled life, cities, civilisations, and eventually modern society.

✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Paleolithic (~2M-10,000 BCE) → Mesolithic (~10,000-6,000 BCE) → Neolithic (~6,000-2,500 BCE) → Chalcolithic (~2,500-700 BCE). From hunting-gathering to farming to metal tools.

Q9. Which of the following is NOT a feature of the Paleolithic Age?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Agriculture and settled villages are Neolithic features, NOT Paleolithic. Paleolithic = hunting-gathering, rough stone tools, cave paintings (Bhimbetka), nomadic lifestyle.
Q10. Which of the following is a key Neolithic site in Bihar?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Chirand (Saran district, Bihar, on the Ganga) is the most important prehistoric site in Bihar. Stone + copper tools, pottery, rice cultivation, bone tools, burial sites. Continuous occupation from Neolithic to Chalcolithic.
Q11. The term 'microlith' refers to:
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Microliths are small stone tools (1-5 cm) characteristic of the Mesolithic Age. Types: arrowheads, blades, points, lunates. Made from flint, chert, agate. Often hafted onto wood/bone handles as composite tools.
Q12. Which age is known for the beginning of agriculture in India?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — The Neolithic Age (~6,000 BCE) is known for the beginning of agriculture — the 'Neolithic Revolution.' First crops: wheat and barley (Mehrgarh ~7000 BCE). Rice cultivation began later in the Ganga valley.
Q13. Which prehistoric site in Kashmir is known for pit dwellings?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Burzahom (Kashmir, ~3000 BCE) is a Neolithic site known for pit dwellings — houses built in pits (for insulation against cold). Also: polished stone tools, bone tools, barley cultivation, dog burials.
Q14. Which metal was first used by humans in India?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Copper was the first metal used in India (Chalcolithic Age, ~2,500 BCE). Copper is found in a relatively pure state and is easy to work. Bronze (copper+tin) was used by the IVC. Iron came ~1,000 BCE.
Q15. The Iron Age in India began approximately in which period?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — The Iron Age began ~1,000 BCE in India. Iron revolutionised agriculture (ploughshares) and warfare (weapons). Associated with PGW culture, later Vedic period, rise of Mahajanapadas, second urbanisation.
Q16. Which of the following correctly matches the site with its state?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Bhimbetka is in Madhya Pradesh. Correct matches: Bhimbetka-MP, Mehrgarh-Balochistan, Burzahom-Kashmir, Chirand-Bihar.
Q17. The people of the Paleolithic Age survived primarily by:
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Paleolithic people survived by hunting wild animals and gathering wild fruits, roots, honey. They were nomadic — moving in search of food. Agriculture and domestication came in the Neolithic Age.
Q18. Which period is associated with the 'Neolithic Revolution'?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — The Neolithic Revolution (term by Gordon Childe) = transition from hunting-gathering to farming (~6,000 BCE in India). Most important revolution in human history — led to settled life, population growth, surplus food, urbanisation.
Q19. Which of the following was a feature of the Chalcolithic Age but NOT of the Neolithic Age?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Copper tools alongside stone tools = Chalcolithic feature (not Neolithic). Agriculture, domestication, and polished stone tools were Neolithic features that continued into the Chalcolithic Age.
Q20. Which archaeological site in Bihar shows continuous occupation from the Neolithic to the Chalcolithic period?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Chirand (Saran district, Bihar, on the Ganga) shows continuous occupation from Neolithic to Chalcolithic. Stone + copper tools, pottery, rice cultivation, bone tools, burial sites. Most important prehistoric site in Bihar.
Q21. The rock paintings at Bhimbetka depict:
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Bhimbetka paintings depict hunting scenes, dancing, animals (elephants, deer, tigers, boars), and daily life of prehistoric people. 750+ rock shelters, spanning Paleolithic to Mesolithic. UNESCO World Heritage Site (2003).
Q22. Who discovered the Indus Valley site of Harappa in 1921?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Daya Ram Sahni discovered Harappa (1921). R.D. Banerjee discovered Mohenjodaro (1922). John Marshall (DG, ASI) announced the IVC in 1924. IVC dated 2,500-1,750 BCE.
Q23. Which of the following statements about prehistoric Bihar is correct?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Chirand (Saran district, Bihar, on the Ganga) is an important Neolithic site. Stone tools, copper tools, pottery, rice cultivation, bone tools. Most important prehistoric site in Bihar. Other sites: Senuar, Chechar-Kutubpur.
Q24. The transition from the Chalcolithic Age to the Iron Age in India is associated with which pottery culture?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Painted Grey Ware (PGW) culture (~1,200-600 BCE) marks the transition from Chalcolithic to Iron Age. Grey pottery with painted designs. Associated with later Vedic period. Key sites: Hastinapur, Atranjikhera, Ahichchhatra.
Q25. The Black and Red Ware pottery is a characteristic of which age in India?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Black and Red Ware (BRW) is characteristic of the Chalcolithic Age (~2,500-700 BCE). Black interior, red exterior (inverted firing). Found at Ahar (Rajasthan), Malwa (MP), Jorwe (Maharashtra), Chirand (Bihar).
