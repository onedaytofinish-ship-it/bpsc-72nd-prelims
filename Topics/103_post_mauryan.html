Post-Mauryan — Sungas, Kushanas, Satavahanas

EXAM CORE Post-Mauryan India (c. 185 BCE – 320 CE): political fragmentation, economic boom. Five blocs — Shungas (Pataliputra), Indo-Greeks (Menander), Shakas (Ujjain, Rudradaman I), Kushanas (Purushapura and Mathura, Kanishka), Satavahanas (Deccan, Gautamiputra Satakarni). Firsts: portrait and gold coins, the first long inscription in chaste Sanskrit (150 CE), the first Buddha images, the first land grants to monks, and the Saka era of 78 CE.

A. The Frame — Fragmentation Without Decline

These centuries were once called a dark age of invasion. That reading is rejected: unity broke, but coinage multiplied in five metals, guilds became bankers, and India joined the Silk Route and the monsoon sea route. Two ideas carry the questions. First, the "foreigners" were absorbed — Greeks, Shakas, Parthians and Kushanas took Indian names, titles and gods, and the law-books classed them as lapsed Kshatriyas. Second, the political centre moved out of the middle Ganga plain to Ujjain, Mathura, Purushapura and Pratishthana, returning to Magadha only with the Guptas.

B. The Shungas (c. 185–73 BCE) — Coup and Brahmanical Revival

Around 185 BCE the Mauryan commander-in-chief Pushyamitra Shunga, a Brahmana, killed the last Maurya, Brihadratha, at an army review — the story is told in Bana's Harshacharita. He kept Pataliputra as capital, with Vidisha a second seat under his son Agnimitra, hero of Kalidasa's Malavikagnimitram. The last Shunga, Devabhuti, was killed by his minister Vasudeva after 112 years of the dynasty.

  • The Ayodhya inscription of Dhanadeva calls Pushyamitra dvir-ashvamedha-yajin, performer of two horse sacrifices — the epigraphic proof of the ashvamedha revival after Ashoka's dhamma.
  • Patanjali's Mahabhashya, the great commentary on Panini's Ashtadhyayi, says "here we perform the sacrifice for Pushyamitra" and notes a Yavana siege of Saketa — placing Patanjali in this court in the 2nd century BCE.
  • Bharhut's gateway records a gift "during the rule of the Shungas" (Suganam raje), and Sanchi Stupa No. 1 was encased in stone in the same age — proof that Buddhist building continued under Shunga rule. Both show the Buddha only by symbols: footprints, empty throne, wheel, Bodhi tree — the last phase of aniconic art.

D. Kanvas and the Cheti Line of Kalinga

The Kanvas (c. 73–28 BCE), founded by Vasudeva, were four Brahmana kings over a shrunken Magadha; the Puranas say the Satavahanas ended them. Far more examinable is Kharavela of the Cheti (Chedi) dynasty of Kalinga, known from one document.

E. The Indo-Greeks — Coins, Kings and the Milindapanha

Bactria broke from the Seleucids and pushed into India from about 180 BCE under Demetrius I. Its great ruler was Menander I (c. 155–130 BCE), the Milinda of Indian tradition, ruling from Sakala (Sialkot). The Pali Milindapanha records his dialogue with the monk Nagasena on the self and rebirth; tradition holds he embraced Buddhism, and Plutarch says his ashes were divided among his cities. The Greeks also left the satrap system of provincial governors, continued by the Shakas.

  • Numismatic firsts: the first coins in India assignable with certainty to named kings, the first with a realistic royal portrait, and the first gold coins struck in India — against the anonymous punch-marked coinage before it. Their bilingual legends, Greek on the obverse and Prakrit in Kharoshthi on the reverse, later gave scholars the key to the ancient scripts.
  • Heliodorus pillar, Besnagar (Vidisha, Madhya Pradesh): a Garuda-dhvaja raised c. 113 BCE by Heliodorus, envoy of Antialcidas of Taxila to King Bhagabhadra. He calls himself a Bhagavata and dedicates it to Vasudeva, god of gods — the earliest firm evidence of Vaishnava devotion, and of a Greek adopting it.

F. Shakas and Parthians — Rudradaman and the Sanskrit Turn

The Shakas (Scythians) entered in the 1st century BCE in several branches; the durable one was the Western Kshatrapas of Saurashtra and Malwa, centred on Ujjain. The Parthians (Pahlavas) held a smaller north-western zone; their king Gondophernes is the one at whose court Christian tradition places St Thomas.

The Junagadh text also claims he twice defeated Satakarni, lord of the Dakshinapatha, but spared him for close kinship. Its wider significance: a "foreign" dynasty made Sanskrit the prestige language a century and a half before the Guptas.

G. The Kushanas — Yuezhi Nomads to Silk Route Empire

Kushana gold of Kanishka I, struck near the Roman aureus weight. Trap: gold coins were introduced by the Indo-Greeks, issued in bulk by the Kushanas, perfected by the Guptas. Kanishka's coins carry Greek, Iranian and Indian deities — Oesho (Shiva), Nana and a standing Buddha labelled "Boddo" — so he was not an exclusively Buddhist king. Control of the Silk Route passes brought the customs revenue behind this gold, and carried Buddhism into Khotan, Kashgar and China.

The Kushanas were one of five clans of the Yuezhi of Central Asia; their dynastic order is fixed by the Rabatak inscription (Afghanistan, Bactrian in Greek script).

H. Kanishka, the Fourth Council and the Rise of Mahayana

  • Empire: from Bactria and the Khotan–Kashgar region into the Ganga plain as far as Varanasi. The headless Kanishka statue from Mat near Mathura bears the titles maharaja rajatiraja devaputra — Indian, Iranian and Chinese royal idioms fused.
  • Fourth Buddhist Council: held in his reign in Kashmir under Vasumitra, with Ashvaghosha as deputy; the commentaries (Mahavibhasha) were in Sanskrit, not Pali, and the Hinayana–Mahayana split is conventionally dated here. Theravada tradition does not recognise it.
  • Court circle (traditional): Ashvaghosha, author of the Buddhacharita, the first full Sanskrit life of the Buddha; Vasumitra; Charaka, physician of the Charaka Samhita; and Nagarjuna, founder of the Madhyamika school and its doctrine of shunyata. Nagarjuna is also linked to a Satavahana king, so treat the list as traditional.
  • What Mahayana changed: the Buddha becomes a divine being fit for worship; the goal shifts from the arhat's nirvana to the Bodhisattva ideal of postponing liberation to help all beings; bhakti enters Buddhism; and the Buddha is shown in human form, creating the demand that produced the sculpture schools below.

J. The Satavahanas — the First Great Deccan Power

Called Andhras in the Puranas, they rose as Mauryan control of the Deccan lapsed. Simuka is the named founder, Pratishthana (Paithan) the capital; their duel with the western Shakas defines Deccan politics for two centuries.

Gautamiputra Satakarni (c. 106–130 CE)Destroyed the Shaka Nahapana; the Nashik prashasti of his mother Gautami Balashri calls him eka-brahmana, destroyer of Shakas, Yavanas and Pahlavas, and the king who stopped the mixing of varnas
Vasishthiputra PulumaviIssued that prashasti in his 19th year; married a daughter of Rudradaman I — the diplomacy behind Rudradaman's claim to have spared a Satakarni
  • Matronymics: kings took the mother's name — Gautamiputra, Vasishthiputra. Succession stayed patrilineal, so this signals high status for royal women, not matriarchy. That distinction is the trap.
  • Land grants: the earliest recorded grants of tax-free land in India, to Buddhist monks and Brahmanas, the state giving up its right to enter or tax them — the seed of the later land-grant economy.
  • Language, coins, administration: Prakrit in Brahmi was the court language, against Shaka Sanskrit; coinage was mainly lead and potin, with the ship coins of the last great king, Yajna Sri Satakarni, proving maritime trade; Hala of this line is credited with the Gathasaptashati; provinces were aharas under amatyas, with feudatories ranked raja, mahabhoja and maharathi.
  • Religion: two-handed — Vedic sacrifice and Brahmana agrahara grants alongside the chaityas of Karle, Bhaja and Kanheri and the Amaravati stupa.

K. The Trade Boom — Rome, the Monsoon and the Ports

Around the mid-1st century CE the Greek pilot Hippalus publicised use of the south-west monsoon to sail straight across the Arabian Sea instead of hugging the coast. The anonymous Periplus of the Erythraean Sea maps the network that followed.

  • Exports: pepper (Roman yavanapriya), spices, muslin, pearls, ivory, gems, Chinese silk in transit. Imports: wine, glass, tin, lead, coral — and above all bullion.
  • Pliny the Elder complained in his Natural History of the drain of Roman specie, putting India's annual take at 50 million sesterces and the combined drain to India, China and Arabia at 100 million. Peninsular hoards confirm the flow, and the resulting guild capital paid for the stupas and cave monasteries of the age.

L. Society, Religion and the Guilds as Bankers

  • Guilds (shreni) fixed craft standards and acted as banks: the Nashik inscription of Ushavadata, son-in-law of Nahapana, records permanent endowments deposited with weavers' guilds at Govardhana, the interest to be spent on monks in perpetuity — the earliest clear record of endowment banking in India. Hereditary guilds also hardened into craft castes, a step from varna to jati.
  • Bhagavatism: the Heliodorus pillar, the Ghosundi and Hathibada inscriptions of Rajasthan naming Samkarshana and Vasudeva, and the Naneghat invocation mark the emergence of Vaishnava bhakti — devotion replacing costly sacrifice, open to those barred from Vedic ritual. Shaivism appears on Kushana coins and in Vima Kadphises' titles.
  • First Buddha images appear in the 1st–2nd centuries CE at Mathura and in Gandhara, replacing the symbols of Bharhut and Sanchi. Which came first is still argued; say they developed together under Kushana patronage.
TRAP ALERT Four confusions that cost marks: (1) Hathigumpha = Kharavela, Prakrit, Odisha; Junagadh = Rudradaman, Sanskrit, Gujarat. (2) The Fourth Council was in Kashmir under Kanishka, the Third at Pataliputra under Ashoka. (3) Saka era 78 CE; Vikrama Samvat 58 BCE. (4) Bharhut is in Madhya Pradesh, not Bihar.

🔶 Bihar Connection
Bihar in the Post-Mauryan Centuries — Capital, Then Eclipse
  • Pataliputra (Patna district) stays the capital. The coup of c. 185 BCE was a palace revolution, not conquest from outside: army, treasury and bureaucracy stayed put and the Shungas ruled from the Mauryan capital. Vidisha in Malwa was the second seat — which is why most Shunga art survives in Madhya Pradesh, not Bihar.
  • Patanjali and the sacrificial revival. The Mahabhashya, whose author speaks of performing sacrifice for Pushyamitra, belongs to this court, and the ashvamedhas attested at Ayodhya were staged from the Magadhan capital — so the land where Ashoka proclaimed dhamma became the stage for the most conspicuous Vedic revival in ancient India.
  • The Bharhut trap — Bharhut is NOT in Bihar. The finest Shunga-period Buddhist monument stands in Satna district, Madhya Pradesh; Cunningham found it in 1873 and its railings are now in the Indian Museum, Kolkata. Students assume any early stupa in a Shunga question must be Bihar's, because the Shungas ruled from Patna. Learn the pair: capital in Bihar, stupa in Madhya Pradesh.
  • Bodh Gaya (Gaya district) — the genuine Bihar monument of the age. The stone railing (vedika) round the Mahabodhi shrine is Shunga-period, roughly 1st century BCE, with medallions of lotuses, animals and worship at the Bodhi tree, and donor inscriptions including one naming Kurangi, a royal wife. The Buddha is not in human form here — tree, throne and wheel stand for him.
  • Rajgir and the Barabar hills (Nalanda and Jehanabad districts) under attack. Kharavela's record describes his march to Gorathagiri — generally identified with the Barabar range in Jehanabad district — and to Rajagriha, then deeper into Magadha. Within a century Bihar had gone from invading Kalinga to being raided by it. Note that the Barabar and Nagarjuni caves themselves are Mauryan — Ashoka's and Dasharatha Maurya's gifts to the Ajivikas — so dating them to the Shungas or Kushanas is wrong.
  • Magadha's primacy ends here — the chronology students get wrong. After the Kanvas fall around 28 BCE, no imperial dynasty rules from Pataliputra for some three and a half centuries; power sits at Ujjain, Purushapura, Mathura and Pratishthana. The Kushanas did not rule Bihar — their Ganga reach stopped near Varanasi. Bihar becomes a land of small local rulers, of Mitra chiefs known from coins, and of a re-emergent Lichchhavi presence around Vaishali (Vaishali district).
  • Centrality returns only with the Guptas, c. 320 CE. The Lichchhavi link is the bridge — Chandragupta I's marriage to Kumaradevi is commemorated on his coins — and Pataliputra becomes imperial again. The sequence is Mauryan peak → Shunga continuity → long eclipse from the Kanvas to the Guptas → Gupta revival; any option saying Bihar was continuously India's political centre from Ashoka to the Guptas is false. Bodh Gaya, Rajgir and Vaishali still drew monks throughout, which is why Nalanda was founded here in the Gupta age.

📰 Current Relevance
  • Saka Era (78 CE): the official calendar of India — used by the Government of India for the National Calendar. 2026 CE = 1948-49 Saka. Started by Kanishka's accession.
  • Silk Route: the Kushanas controlled the ancient Silk Route (China to Rome). Today, China's 'Belt and Road Initiative' (BRI) evokes the same route — connecting Asia, Europe, and Africa.
  • Gandhara art: the first human images of the Buddha (Gandhara + Mathura schools, Kushana period) — now in museums worldwide. The Bamiyan Buddhas (Afghanistan, destroyed by Taliban in 2001) were also Kushana-era.
  • Milinda Panha: the dialogue between Indo-Greek king Menander and Buddhist monk Nagasena — a classic of Buddhist philosophy, still studied today.

Q1. Who killed the last Mauryan emperor Brihadratha and founded the Shunga dynasty?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Pushyamitra Shunga, a Brahmin general, killed Brihadratha c. 185 BCE. Founded Shunga dynasty. Patron of Brahminical culture, performed Ashvamedha.
Q2. The Shunga dynasty ruled from which capital?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Shungas ruled from Pataliputra (Bihar) — continuing the Mauryan capital. Pushyamitra (c. 185-149 BCE). Extended from Punjab to Bengal, south to Narmada.
Q3. Pushyamitra Shunga was a patron of which religion?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Pushyamitra patronised Brahminical Hinduism. Performed Ashvamedha (Vedic horse sacrifice). Buddhist texts accuse him of persecuting Buddhists (debated). Brahminical revival.
Q4. The famous Bharhut Stupa (with magnificent Buddhist sculptures) was built during which period?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Bharhut Stupa (Madhya Pradesh) built/enlarged during Shunga period (c. 2nd c. BCE). Buddhist sculptures (railings, gateways) with inscriptions. Now in Indian Museum, Kolkata.
Q5. Patanjali, the famous grammarian who wrote the Mahabhashya (commentary on Panini's grammar), lived during which period?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Patanjali lived during the Shunga period (c. 2nd c. BCE), contemporary of Pushyamitra. Mahabhashya = greatest commentary on Panini's Ashtadhyayi. Also Yoga Sutras (possibly different Patanjali).
Q6. After the decline of the Mauryan Empire, which foreign invaders entered India from the northwest?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Indo-Greeks (Bactrian Greeks) invaded c. 180 BCE. Most famous: Menander I (Milinda, c. 165-130 BCE) — ruled from Punjab, possibly converted to Buddhism (Milinda Panha).
Q7. The most famous Indo-Greek king, who ruled from Punjab and possibly converted to Buddhism, was:
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Menander I (Milinda, c. 165-130 BCE). Ruled from Sakala (Sialkot, Punjab). Milinda Panha records dialogue with monk Nagasena. Possible Buddhist conversion.
Q8. The Indo-Greeks introduced which innovation to Indian coinage?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Indo-Greeks introduced gold/silver coins with realistic portraits + bilingual legends (Greek on obverse, Kharoshthi/Prakrit on reverse). Earliest Indian coins with portraits.
Q9. The Kushanas were a branch of which nomadic tribe?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Kushanas = branch of Yuezhi (Yueh-chi) from Central Asia (modern Xinjiang/Gansu, China). Migrated west, displaced Indo-Greeks, established Kushana Empire (c. 1st-3rd c. CE). A Central Asian nomadic tribe.
Q10. Who was the most famous Kushana emperor, known for the Fourth Buddhist Council and the Saka Era?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Kanishka (c. 78 CE). Fourth Buddhist Council (Kashmir, 72 CE). Saka Era (78 CE). Empire from Central Asia to Varanasi. Gandhara + Mathura art. Silk Route.
Q11. The Fourth Buddhist Council was held under Kanishka's patronage at which place?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Fourth Council: Kashmir (c. 72 CE), Kanishka. Presided by Vasumitra (deputy Ashvaghosha). Split: Hinayana (Theravada) vs Mahayana. Sanskrit became Buddhist scholarly language.
Q12. The Saka Era (starting 78 CE) is associated with which ruler?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Saka Era (78 CE) = Kanishka's accession. Official calendar of India (National Calendar). 2026 CE = 1948-49 Saka. Still used by Government of India. C. 78 CE, the Saka Era is traditionally linked to his accession.
Q13. The famous Silk Route trade flourished under which empire?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Kushanas controlled the Silk Route (China to Rome via Central Asia). Prospered from trade (silk, spices, gold, gems). Kanishka's empire extended to Central Asia (Bactria, Kashgar, Yarkand).
Q14. The Gandhara School of Art (Indo-Greek Buddhist art) flourished under which empire?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Gandhara School flourished under Kushanas (1st-3rd c. CE) in Gandhara region (Peshawar, Taxila). Greco-Roman + Indian styles. First human images of Buddha (Hellenistic features). C. 1st-3rd century CE, in modern Pakistan/Afghanistan.
Q15. The Mathura School of Art (indigenous Indian Buddhist art) also flourished under the Kushanas. Where is Mathura?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Mathura School at Mathura (UP, south of Delhi). Indigenous Indian style (unlike Greco-Roman Gandhara). Buddha with Indian features (usnisa, serene, transparent robes). Also Jain + Hindu images. In modern Uttar Pradesh, south of Delhi.
Q16. Ashvaghosha, the famous Buddhist scholar who wrote the Buddhacharita (a biography of the Buddha in Sanskrit), lived during which period?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Ashvaghosha = Kanishka's court poet. Wrote Buddhacharita (first biography of the Buddha, in Sanskrit). Also Sutralankara. Deputy president of Fourth Buddhist Council.
Q17. The Satavahana dynasty ruled which region of India (c. 1st century BCE - 3rd century CE)?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Satavahanas ruled the Deccan (Maharashtra, Telangana, AP, Karnataka). Capital: Pratishthana (Paithan, Maharashtra). First major Deccan empire. Founder: Simuka. Greatest: Gautamiputra Satakarni. Modern Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka.
Q18. The greatest Satavahana king, who defeated the Shaka ruler Nahapana and extended the empire, was:
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Gautamiputra Satakarni (c. 106-130 CE). Defeated Shaka Nahapana. Extended Malwa to Karnataka. Mother Gautami Balasri's Nashik inscription. 'Destroyer of the pride of the Kshaharatas.'
Q19. The Satavahanas were known for which unique administrative feature?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Satavahanas used matronymics: Gautamiputra ('son of Gautami'), Vasishtiputra, Pulumavi. High status for women. Women donated land and made religious gifts. Kings used their mother's name, e.g., Gautamiputra = 'son of Gautami'.
Q20. The Satavahanas issued which type of coins primarily?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Satavahanas issued lead, potin (copper alloy), copper coins — unique to Deccan, for local trade. Also some silver/gold (imitating Indo-Greek/Shaka styles). Lead coins: elephants, horses, Ujjain symbols.
Q21. The Satavahanas patronised which religions?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Satavahanas patronised both Buddhism and Brahminical Hinduism. Land grants (agraharas) to Brahmins, donations to Buddhist monasteries. Amaravati Stupa, Nashik caves. Religious eclecticism.
Q22. The Shaka (Western Kshatrapa) dynasty ruled which region?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Shakas (Western Kshatrapas) ruled Saurashtra (Gujarat) and Malwa (western India), c. 1st-4th c. CE. Capital: Ujjain. Famous ruler: Rudradaman I (Junagadh inscription, 150 CE).
Q23. Rudradaman I, the famous Shaka ruler, is known for which inscription?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Rudradaman's Junagadh Rock Inscription (c. 150 CE, Gujarat) = first long inscription in pure Sanskrit (earlier were Prakrit). Records repair of Sudarshana Lake (built by Chandragupta Maurya, enhanced by Ashoka). Defeated Satavahanas twice.
Q24. Bihar's connection with the Post-Mauryan period is primarily through which fact?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Shungas ruled from Pataliputra (Bihar) — the Mauryan capital. Pushyamitra Shunga controlled Bihar, Bengal, central India. After Shungas, Bihar came under the Guptas (c. 320 CE). Kushanas ruled northwest, not Bihar directly.
Q25. Which of the following is NOT a feature of the Post-Mauryan period (c. 185 BCE - 320 CE)?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Post-Mauryan period was NOT a single unified empire — fragmented: Shungas (north), Indo-Greeks (northwest), Shakas (west), Kushanas (northwest+north), Satavahanas (Deccan). Unlike the unified Mauryan Empire.
