Phase 1: Pre-1857 Tribal & Peasant Resistance

Santhal Hool (1855–56)

AspectDetail
LeadersSidho and Kanhu Murmu (brothers)
RegionRajmahal hills, Bhagalpur-Birbhum (then in Bengal Presidency, now partly Jharkhand)
CausesZamindari oppression, moneylender (mahajan) exploitation, land alienation, corrupt police and judicial system
Key event30 June 1855 — Sidho and Kanhu declared rebellion; gathered ~10,000 Santhals; attacked zamindars, moneylenders, and police stations
SuppressionBritish declared martial law; used military force; Sidho killed in battle (Aug 1855), Kanhu captured and executed (Feb 1856)
OutcomeSanthal Pargana district created (1855) as a separate non-regulation area to protect tribals; but the structural causes persisted
SignificanceOne of India's earliest organised tribal rebellions; pre-dates 1857 by two years; commemorated as Hool Diwas (30 June)
70th PYQ Q108: "Santhal Hool (Rebellion) took place in?" → 1855–56. BPSC tests the year and the leaders (Sidho & Kanhu Murmu). Note: the Hool is distinct from Birsa Munda's revolt (1895–1900, Chhotanagpur) and from Tilka Manjhi's earlier Santhal uprising (1784).

Why Kunwar Singh is Bihar's 1857 icon:
At 80 years old, Kunwar Singh was the oldest rebel leader of 1857. He fought across three provinces (Bihar, UP, MP) — a remarkable feat for his age. His decision to cut off his wounded hand and offer it to the Ganga became a symbol of defiance. His brother Amar Singh continued the fight for nearly a year after his death. The Veer Kunwar Singh University at Ara and the Veer Kunwar Singh Setu (bridge) are named after him.

Phase 4: Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — Deep Dive

The Tinkathia System

  • What: European indigo planters forced Champaran peasants to cultivate indigo on 3/20 (15%) of their land — hence "tin-kathia" (three kathas out of twenty).
  • How: The system was enforced through coercion — peasants who refused were beaten, fined, or had their land seized. The planters set the price for indigo, which was below the market rate for food crops.
  • Why indigo declined: By 1917, German synthetic indigo had made natural indigo unprofitable. The planters kept forcing the system anyway, extracting compensation from peasants who wanted to stop — a double exploitation.
  • Peasant grievances: Fraudulent contracts, illegal enhancement of rent, arbitrary fines, "abwabs" (illegal cesses), and physical intimidation by planters' agents (naibs).

Why Champaran matters: It was Gandhi's first satyagraha in India (his earlier satyagrahas were in South Africa). The method — arrive, survey, document, refuse to leave, negotiate from a position of moral authority — became the template for all future Gandhian movements. Champaran established Gandhi as a national leader and proved that peaceful mass resistance could force the colonial government to act.

Phase 6: Quit India 1942 — Bihar Hotspot Deep Dive

JP's Hazaribagh Jail Escape

  • Context: After the Quit India resolution (8 Aug 1942) and mass arrests (9 Aug 1942), JP was imprisoned in Hazaribagh Central Jail (then Bihar, now Jharkhand).
  • Escape: November 1942 — JP escaped along with fellow prisoners by scaling the jail wall. The escape was meticulously planned and executed during a Diwali night when firecrackers masked the noise.
  • Aftermath: JP went underground, travelled across Bihar organising the resistance, established contact with underground leaders in Bombay and Delhi. He was finally arrested in September 1943 at Kanpur.
  • Legacy: The escape became one of the most celebrated events of Quit India — JP was called "Lok Nayak" (People's Leader) for his daring and leadership.
