A. Raid Converted into State

The Sultanate (1206–1526) shows how Turkish military slaves turned raids into a state with a salaried army, a measured land tax and a currency. Three structural facts explain most of it. There was no law of succession, so the throne went to whoever held the army at Delhi — hence the coups after Aibak, Iltutmish, Balban and Alauddin. Sovereignty was borrowed: legitimacy rested on the khutba (sermon in the Sultan's name), the sikka (coins in his name) and the Caliph's patent. The Mongol threat was permanent from the 1220s to about 1330 — Alauddin's price control existed to pay the army that met it.

C. The Slave / Mamluk Dynasty (1206–1290)

Qutbuddin Aibak (1206–1210), a Turkish slave of Muhammad Ghori, ruled from Lahore and was called Lakh Baksh, "giver of lakhs". Note the qualification: he founded the state but never struck coins or read the khutba in his name. He began the Qutub Minar and built Quwwat-ul-Islam at Delhi and Adhai Din ka Jhonpra at Ajmer, both from temple masonry, and died in 1210 falling from his horse at chaugan (polo).

Iltutmish (1211–1236) is the real consolidator and owns the "firsts": capital moved from Lahore to Delhi; slave officers organised into the Turkan-i-Chihalgani, the corps of forty; the iqta formalised — a tract's revenue assigned to an officer instead of salary, against maintaining troops; the silver tanka (about 175 grains) and copper jital issued. In 1229 the Caliph al-Mustansir Billah sent a patent of investiture, making Delhi a legally recognised sultanate. His shrewdest act was a refusal: in 1221 he denied shelter to the Khwarizmian prince Jalaluddin Mangbarni, keeping Chengiz Khan out of the Indian plains.

Qutub Minar, Delhi (UNESCO, 1993), about 73 m — the world's tallest brick minaret. Trap: begun by Aibak (c. 1199), completed by Iltutmish (1231–32). The adjoining Quwwat-ul-Islam uses corbelled, not true, arches — the true arch and dome arrive with the Alai Darwaza of 1311.

Razia (1236–1240) is the only woman ever to rule Delhi, and used the title Sultan. Her fall was political: she discarded the veil, held open court and made the Abyssinian Jalaluddin Yaqut amir-i-akhur (master of the stables), breaking the Turkish monopoly on office. The Forty and the theologians combined against her; Altunia of Bhatinda revolted; she married him and both were killed in 1240 — proof that the Forty could unmake sovereigns.

Balban (1266–1287), himself one of the Forty, destroyed that oligarchy through an explicit theory of kingship: the Sultan is zill-i-Ilahi, the shadow of God, exercising niyabat-i-khudai, divine deputyship second only to prophethood. He enforced sijda (prostration) and paibos (kissing the feet), kept a wineless court and barred low-born men from office. His blood-and-iron policy crushed the Mewatis and Tughril's revolt in Bengal, and he rebuilt the diwan-i-arz under a separate ariz-i-mumalik, taking the army from the wazir.

D. The Khaljis (1290–1320)

Jalaluddin Firoz Khalji took the throne in 1290 at about seventy, ending the Ilbari Turkish monopoly, and ruled with a mildness his nobles read as weakness. In 1296 his nephew and son-in-law Alauddin, governor of Kara, raided Devagiri, returned with its treasure and had him killed. Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316), styled Sikandar-i-Sani, took Gujarat (1299), Ranthambore (1301), Chittor (1303), Malwa (1305) and Jalor (1311); in the south Malik Kafur reduced Devagiri, Warangal, Dwarasamudra and Madurai (1307–1311) — the first Delhi push into the far south, and these became tributaries. He beat off repeated Mongol invasions, notably Amroha (1305). Barani says his four ordinances made conspiracy impossible: religious grants confiscated; a spy service of barids and munhiyan; wine prohibited; nobles' assemblies, intermarriage and private visits banned.

E. Alauddin's Market Control — the Set-Piece Answer

Alauddin needed a very large standing army against the Mongols but could not afford it at market wages — Barani puts a cavalryman's pay at 234 tankas a year, 78 more for a second horse. Rather than raise pay he forced prices down, fixing rates at the point of production, not in the shop alone.

  • Diwan-i-riyasat — the commerce department over all four markets (Barani names Yaqub Nazir as controller), with the shahna-i-mandi beneath it.
  • Anti-hoarding: regrating (ihtikar) was banned and Delhi's granaries filled with revenue grain from the Doab, released when supply fell.
  • Double reporting: market officers, barids and secret munhiyan reported prices separately, so false returns were detectable.
  • Barani's stated reason is military — cheap prices meant a big army on a small budget; historians add inflation control. It worked mainly around Delhi and lapsed at his death.

The revenue side made this possible. Land was measured (masahat) in biswa units and the demand fixed at half the produce — the highest in Sultanate history — taken in kind in the Doab. He stripped the intermediaries (khuts, muqaddams, chaudhuris) of their exemptions, making them pay the grazing tax (charai) and house tax (ghari) like ordinary peasants, so the burden could not be pushed down. He was the first Sultan with a large standing army paid in cash, policed by dagh (horse-branding) and chehra (soldiers' rolls).

Chittorgarh, Rajasthan — taken by Alauddin Khalji in 1303, renamed Khizrabad after his son Khizr Khan. Trap: the Padmini story comes from Jayasi's Padmavat, an Awadhi Sufi allegory of about 1540; the contemporary account, Khusrau's Khazain-ul-Futuh, never mentions her.

F. The Tughlaqs (1320–1414)

Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq (1320–1325), the frontier warden Ghazi Malik, restored order, built Tughlaqabad and moderated the land tax; he died in 1325 when a wooden pavilion collapsed on him at Afghanpur. Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351) was the most learned man to hold the throne — mathematician, logician, calligrapher — and the most damaging administrator, a mixture of opposites: his schemes were sound in conception but imposed too fast, over too large an area, untested.

The result was disintegration: Madurai broke away (1335), Bengal followed (1338), Vijayanagara arose in 1336 and the Bahmani kingdom in 1347. He died in Sindh in 1351 — Barani's verdict: the king was freed from his people and they from their king. Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388) then bought stability from the nobility and ulama at the cost of the state's long-term strength.

  • Canals: chiefly Yamuna–Hisar and Sutlej–Ghaggar, with an irrigation cess (haqq-i-sharb) of one-tenth.
  • New towns: Firozabad, Hisar, Fatehabad, Firozpur and Jaunpur (1359), named for Muhammad bin Tughlaq, whose name was Jauna.
  • Sharia taxation: only kharaj, zakat, jizya, khams, other cesses abolished — but he was the first Sultan to extend jizya to brahmins, till then exempt.
  • Welfare: a diwan-i-khairat for marriages of poor Muslim girls, a darul-shafa (free hospital) and an employment bureau.
  • Antiquarian work: he repaired the Qutub Minar, had two Ashokan pillars brought to Delhi, from Topra (Haryana) and Meerut, and wrote Futuhat-i-Firozshahi.
  • The fatal concessions: iqtas and army service made hereditary, plus a slave department (diwan-i-bandagan) of some 180,000 — undoing Alauddin's professional service.

In 1398 Timur invaded, beat Sultan Nasiruddin Mahmud Tughlaq and sacked Delhi, taking treasure and artisans to Samarkand, and left Khizr Khan at Multan.

G. Sayyids (1414–1451) and Lodis (1451–1526)

Khizr Khan took Delhi in 1414 but never claimed the title of Sultan, governing as a Timurid deputy with the khutba in their name — a deputyship over a shrunken territory, ended when Alam Shah withdrew to Badaun in 1451. Bahlul Lodi (1451–1489) founded the first Afghan dynasty of Delhi, treated his nobles as tribal equals and annexed Sharqi Jaunpur in 1484. Sikandar Lodi (1489–1517) was the ablest: he founded Agra in 1504 as his capital, standardised measurement with the gaz-i-Sikandari, wrote verse as Gulrukhi and pushed Lodi power into Bihar. Ibrahim Lodi (1517–1526) swapped that tribal style for absolutism; the alienated Afghan nobles and Daulat Khan Lodi of Punjab invited Babur. At the First Battle of Panipat, 21 April 1526, Babur's field artillery and tulughma flanking destroyed a far larger army, and Ibrahim became the only Delhi Sultan to die on a battlefield.

The iqta is the thread of the chapter: the revenue of an area assigned to an officer (muqti, iqtadar) in lieu of salary, against maintaining troops and remitting the surplus (fawazil) to the centre. It was not land ownership and not hereditary, and the holder was transferable — the point of the system. Iltutmish systematised it; Alauddin pulled much of the Doab into khalisa (crown land) and paid soldiers in cash; Firoz Shah made iqtas hereditary, turning a service assignment into property and hollowing out the army. The Mughal jagir descends from it.

Provincially the province was itself an iqta, split into shiqs under a shiqdar, then parganas under an amil, and finally the village, where the hereditary khut, muqaddam and patwari collected. The Sultanate taxed through this village layer rather than displacing it — hence Alauddin's need to break the intermediaries' privileges.

I. Society, Economy and Culture

Architecture shows the synthesis most clearly. Indian building was trabeate — beams and corbelled courses; Islamic building was arcuate — the true arch of wedge-shaped voussoirs locked by a keystone, and the true dome, in lime mortar. The earliest Sultanate buildings are hybrids: Islamic plans executed by Indian masons still corbelling. The Alai Darwaza (1311) is conventionally India's first true dome and arch. Tughlaq work then swung to austerity: sloping "battered" walls, grey rubble, no ornament.

  • Language: Persian became the language of administration, poetry and history into the nineteenth century; mixed with Delhi speech it produced early Hindavi, ancestor of Hindi-Urdu.
  • Sufism: the Chishti order (Muinuddin Chishti at Ajmer, Bakhtiyar Kaki and Nizamuddin Auliya at Delhi, Baba Farid in Punjab) shunned the court and state grants; the Suhrawardi order in Multan took office and wealth; the Firdausi order led in Bihar.
  • Economy: urban growth, a cash-using town economy, trade financed by Multani and Sahu bankers using the hundi, and the spinning wheel and paper-making spreading.
  • Society: the ruling class was an immigrant Turkish, later Afghan, military elite; Indian Muslims and converts were kept from the highest offices.

🔶 Bihar Connection
Bihar under the Sultanate — Conquest, Province, Sufi Centre
  • Bakhtiyar Khalji's conquest, c. 1200–1203: Ikhtiyaruddin Muhammad bin Bakhtiyar Khalji, an officer under Aibak holding iqtas on the Bihar–Awadh border, raided eastward and stormed the fortified monastery at Odantapuri (today Bihar Sharif, Nalanda district). Minhaj-us-Siraj's Tabaqat-i-Nasiri, the only near-contemporary account, says the defenders proved to be shaven-headed "brahmans" — Buddhist monks — and that a great library was found.
  • The monastic universities fall: Odantapuri (Nalanda district), Vikramshila (excavated at Antichak, Bhagalpur district) and Nalanda (Nalanda district) were sacked or abandoned in the same phase. The Tibetan pilgrim Dharmasvamin, at Nalanda in the 1230s, found it deserted but for a few students under Rahula Shribhadra.
  • The single most examinable Bihar fact: this is when Bihar's organised Buddhist institutional life ends. Monastic Buddhism depended on Pala endowments and large residential establishments; once these went, monks migrated to Nepal and Tibet and Buddhism never revived as an institution in the province of its birth. Bodh Gaya (Gaya) survived as pilgrimage; scholarly monasticism did not.
  • Why the province is called "Bihar": from vihara, a monastery — chroniclers called the monastic fort "Bihar", and the building's name became the territory's. The state is named after the institution destroyed in its conquest.
  • Bihar as an iqta: the conquered tract became a province with headquarters at Bihar Sharif (Nalanda district), later Patna, on the corridor between Delhi and Bengal. Whenever Delhi weakened its governors asserted autonomy or drifted towards Bengal, where Bakhtiyar's successors set up a short-lived Khalji line at Lakhnauti (Gaur).
  • Every eastern campaign passed through Bihar: Balban's expedition against the rebel governor Tughril and Firoz Shah Tughlaq's Bengal expeditions of the 1350s moved up the Ganga through Bihar. On that route Firoz founded Jaunpur (1359), soon the Sharqi capital governing Bihar for much of the fifteenth century.
  • Bihar Sharif as a Sufi centre: leading seat of the Firdausi order under Makhdum Sharfuddin Ahmad Yahya Maneri (c. 1263–1381), honoured as Makhdum-ul-Mulk. Born to a scholarly family of Maner (Patna district) — site of the Bari Dargah, which made the Patna–Maner stretch of the Ganga a religious node — he settled at Bihar Sharif, where his dargah on Pir Pahari is still a major shrine with a large annual urs; his Persian Maktubat are important Sufi writings.
  • Hajipur (Vaishali district) is traditionally traced to the mid-fourteenth-century Bengal Sultan Haji Ilyas Shah — evidence of how contested Bihar stayed between Delhi and Gaur.
  • The Lodi recovery: Bahlul Lodi's annexation of Jaunpur (1484) brought Bihar back within Delhi's reach, and Sikandar Lodi campaigned in Bihar in the 1490s, driving the Sharqi fugitive Husain Shah eastward. Afghan clans then took root in south Bihar — including the family holding Sasaram (Rohtas district) that produced Sher Shah Suri.

📰 Current Relevance
  • Qutub Minar: UNESCO World Heritage Site (1993) — one of the most visited monuments in India. 73m (239 ft) tall, started by Qutbuddin Aibak, completed by Iltutmish.
  • Indo-Islamic architecture: the Delhi Sultanate introduced the Indo-Islamic architectural style — blending Islamic arches/domes with Indian carving techniques. Key monuments: Qutub Minar, Alai Darwaza, Begumpuri Mosque, Tughlaqabad Fort.
  • Hindustani language: the Delhi Sultanate period saw the development of Hindustani (Hindi-Urdu) — a blend of Sanskrit-based Prakrit and Persian/Arabic. Amir Khusrau (1253-1325) was a key figure in this development.
  • Destruction of Nalanda: the destruction of Nalanda University by Bakhtiyar Khalji remains a deeply emotional event — UNESCO declared the ruins of Nalanda a World Heritage Site (2016).

✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Slave (1206-1290) → Khalji (1290-1320) → Tughlaq (1320-1414) → Sayyid (1414-1451) → Lodi (1451-1526). 320 years. Mnemonic: S-K-T-S-L.
Q12. Who was the last ruler of the Lodi Dynasty (and the Delhi Sultanate)?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Ibrahim Lodi (1517-1526) — last Lodi Sultan and last Delhi Sultan. Defeated and killed by Babur at Panipat (1526). Arrogant ruler who alienated Afghan nobles.
Q13. The Iqta system of the Delhi Sultanate was:
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Iqta = land revenue assignments to military commanders (iqtadars). Transferable (not hereditary). Introduced by Iltutmish; perfected by Alauddin Khalji. Backbone of Sultanate military-revenue system.
Q14. Who introduced the 'token currency' (copper coins valued as silver) in the Delhi Sultanate?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Muhammad bin Tughlaq introduced token currency (copper/bronze valued as silver). Failed due to forgery — people forged copper coins. Sultan had to withdraw and repay in silver. One of his famous failed experiments.
Q15. Alauddin Khalji's market reforms included:
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Alauddin introduced strict price controls on grain, cloth, sugar, oil, meat. Fixed prices; inspectors; rationing; granaries; punishment for hoarding. Ensured army supply + controlled inflation.
Q16. The Mongol invasions of India during the Delhi Sultanate were successfully resisted by which ruler?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Alauddin defeated the Mongols 6 times — including the Battle of Amroha (1305). Built forts along the northwest frontier. The only Delhi Sultan to decisively defeat the Mongols.
Q17. The Slave Dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate included which famous ruler known as 'Lakh Baksh' (giver of lakhs)?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Qutbuddin Aibak was 'Lakh Baksh' (giver of lakhs) — generous in distributing wealth. First Sultan (1206-1210). Former slave of Ghori. Started Qutub Minar. Died in polo accident.
Q18. Balban (1266-1287 CE) is known for which theory of kingship?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Balban introduced 'divine right' — Sultan = God's shadow (zillullah). Introduced sijda (prostration) and paibos (kissing feet). Suppressed the Chihalgani (Group of Forty).
Q19. Bihar's connection with the Delhi Sultanate was through:
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Bakhtiyar Khalji conquered Bihar (~1200 CE), destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila universities (~1202 CE). Library burned for months. Established Khalji Dynasty of Bengal. Bihar under Muslim rule until 1947.
Q20. The Sayyid Dynasty (1414-1451) claimed descent from which religious figure?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Sayyids claimed descent from Prophet Muhammad (through Fatima and Ali). Founded by Khizr Khan (1414). Weak dynasty — controlled only Delhi. Lasted 37 years.
Q21. Timur (Tamerlane) sacked Delhi in which year, causing massive destruction?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Timur sacked Delhi in 1398 CE — looted for 3 days, killed thousands, destroyed buildings. Weakened the Tughlaq Dynasty — led to the rise of the Sayyid Dynasty (1414). Timur took treasure and artisans to Samarkand.
Q22. Who founded the city of Agra in 1504?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Sikandar Lodi founded Agra (1504) — shifted capital from Delhi. Also conquered Bihar. Patron of learning (poet 'Gulruk'). Son Ibrahim Lodi was last Sultan (defeated at Panipat 1526).
Q23. Which Delhi Sultan shifted the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad (Devagiri)?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Muhammad bin Tughlaq shifted capital to Daulatabad (1327) — to have a more central capital. Ordered entire population of Delhi to move. Caused massive suffering. Reversed after a few years. Famous failed experiment.
Q24. The Chihalgani (Group of Forty) was a powerful group of which type of people in the Delhi Sultanate?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Chihalgani = 40 Turkish slave nobles, originally slaves of Iltutmish. Became kingmakers — installed/deposed Sultans. Finally suppressed by Balban (1266-1287) who saw them as a threat.
Q25. Alauddin Khalji conquered Chittorgarh in 1303 CE. This event is associated with which legend?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Alauddin conquered Chittorgarh (1303). Associated with the legend of Padmini (recorded in Jayasi's Padmavat, 1540). Rajput women committed jauhar (self-immolation) to avoid capture. First of three jauhars at Chittorgarh.
