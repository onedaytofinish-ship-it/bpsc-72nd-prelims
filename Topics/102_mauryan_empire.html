A. The Sources — Reconstructed, Not Simply Read

No Mauryan chronicle survives. The empire is rebuilt from a normative treatise, a lost Greek account, later religious traditions, and the only contemporary voice — the emperor's inscriptions. Knowing each source's limits separates a safe answer from a trap.

B. Chandragupta Maurya (c. 321–297 BCE)

  • Seizure of Magadha: guided by Chanakya, a Brahmin teacher linked to Takshashila, he overthrew the last Nanda (Dhana Nanda) around 321 BCE, took Pataliputra, and expanded west into the vacuum left by Alexander's withdrawal.
  • Contested origins: Buddhist texts make him a Kshatriya of the Moriya clan of Pipphalivana; the Mudrarakshasa's commentator calls him low-born. No source settles it — write "disputed".
  • War with Seleucus Nicator, c. 305–303 BCE: Seleucus tried to recover Alexander's eastern satrapies and was checked. The treaty gave Chandragupta Arachosia (Kandahar), Gedrosia (Baluchistan), Paropamisadae (Kabul valley) and Aria (Herat); Seleucus received 500 war elephants (Plutarch). Strabo mentions a marriage arrangement (epigamia) of debated meaning. Megasthenes arrived as envoy after this settlement.
  • Independent proof of reach: the Junagadh inscription of Rudradaman (150 CE) records the Sudarshana lake in Saurashtra as built by Pushyagupta, Chandragupta's governor, with conduits added by the Yavana Tushaspha under Ashoka.
  • Abdication and sallekhana: Jain tradition holds that he accepted Jainism under Bhadrabahu, abdicated during a twelve-year famine, migrated south and ended his life by sallekhana (fasting to death) at Shravanabelagola, Karnataka — strong tradition, not inscriptional fact.

C. Bindusara (c. 297–273 BCE) — "Amitraghata"

  • Greek writers call him Amitrochates, from Amitraghata, "slayer of foes".
  • Antiochus I Soter of Syria sent the envoy Deimachus; Pliny records that Ptolemy II Philadelphus of Egypt sent Dionysius. Mauryan–Hellenistic diplomacy ran across three reigns.
  • Athenaeus preserves Bindusara's request to Antiochus for sweet wine, dried figs and a sophist; the king sent wine and figs, replying that Greek law forbade selling a philosopher.
  • Tradition records a revolt at Taxila put down by prince Ashoka, and links Bindusara with the Ajivikas.

D. Ashoka (c. 268–232 BCE) — Kalinga and the Meaning of Dhamma

Ashoka calls himself Devanampiya Piyadasi ("Beloved of the Gods, of pleasing appearance") in almost every edict; his personal name appears in only a handful — Maski (the decisive one), Gujarra, Nettur, Udegolam and Panguraria.

The Kalinga war was fought in the 8th year after his consecration, c. 261 BCE. Major Rock Edict XIII gives his own figures — 150,000 deported, 100,000 slain, many times that number dying later — renounces armed conquest for Dhamma-vijaya, and names five Hellenistic kings as recipients of his mission: Antiochus II Theos, Ptolemy II, Antigonus Gonatas, Magas of Cyrene and Alexander of Epirus. Those five names are what allow Ashoka to be dated.

TRAP Rock Edict XIII is deliberately omitted at Dhauli and Jaugada — the two sites inside conquered Kalinga. Two Separate (Kalinga) Rock Edicts were substituted there, addressed to the mahamatras of Tosali and Samapa, ordering that subjects be treated as the king's own children. Ashoka did not advertise his remorse to those he had just conquered.

Dhamma was not Buddhism installed as a state religion. It was a public ethical code for subjects of every sect: obedience to parents, respect for elders and teachers, liberality to Brahmanas and Sramanas alike, kindness to servants and slaves, truthfulness, moderation and restraint from killing. Rock Edict I curbed sacrificial slaughter and festive samajas, noting the royal kitchen cut to two peacocks and a deer a day. Rock Edict XII is the classic statement of religious tolerance: honour other sects and practise restraint of speech (vachaguti).

The Dhamma Mahamatras were a new cadre — part welfare inspectorate, part moral supervisor — working across sects, in frontier regions and among prisoners.

Lion Capital, Sarnath, c. 250 BCE — polished Chunar sandstone: four addorsed lions on an abacus bearing four animals (elephant, bull, horse, lion) separated by four wheels, over an inverted-lotus base. Adopted as the State Emblem of India on 26 January 1950. Traps: only three lions show in the emblem (the fourth is hidden behind); the large crowning Dharma Chakra that once stood above the lions is lost, so the emblem carries only the abacus wheel; and "Satyameva Jayate" comes from the Mundaka Upanishad and is NOT part of the original capital — it was added in Devanagari below the emblem in 1950.

The Rummindei pillar at Lumbini is the most quotable: in his twentieth regnal year Ashoka worshipped there "because here the Buddha, the Sakyamuni, was born", raised a wall and pillar, and made the village udbalike (free of bali) and liable for only one-eighth (athabhagiye) of its produce. It is the inscription that fixes the Buddha's birthplace.

F. Language, Script and Prinsep's Decipherment

  • Language: Prakrit in regional variants, never classical Sanskrit.
  • Scripts: Brahmi across most of the subcontinent; Kharoshthi (right to left) at Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra; Greek and Aramaic at Kandahar (the Shar-i-Kuna bilingual), Aramaic also at Taxila and Laghman.
  • James Prinsep, Secretary of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, deciphered Brahmi in 1837, reading edicts of a king styled Devanampiya Piyadasi. Identifying that title with Ashoka depended on the Sri Lankan chronicles, and was put beyond doubt when the Maski edict, found in 1915, spelt out "Devanampiya Asoka". Before 1837 Ashoka was a name in Buddhist legend; after it, the best-documented ruler of ancient India — which is why the edicts, not the Arthashastra, are the backbone of this topic.

G. Administration — Centre, Provinces and Control

The Mauryan state is India's first sustained attempt at centralised imperial administration — though Romila Thapar argues that real control varied sharply between the metropolitan core, the settled provinces and the forested peripheries.

Megasthenes on the capital: municipal government ran through six committees of five, thirty members in all — crafts, foreigners, registration of births and deaths, trade, inspection of goods, and the sales tax — with a parallel six-fold war office (infantry, cavalry, chariots, elephants, navy, commissariat). Pliny, drawing on him, gives the army as about 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry and 9,000 elephants.

TRAP Megasthenes' "seven castes" — philosophers, farmers, herdsmen, artisans, soldiers, overseers, councillors — is not the varna system and is rejected as inaccurate: it confuses occupation with caste and is probably modelled on Herodotus' account of Egypt. He also claimed India had no slavery, which the Arthashastra's rules on dasas contradict.

H. Economy — Revenue, State Enterprise, Coinage

  • Bhaga — the king's share of produce, conventionally one-sixth, though the Arthashastra allows more in emergency. Bali was an additional levy, once voluntary, now regular; also kara, hiranya (cash) and a water rate.
  • Sita lands were crown lands farmed directly by the state under the sitadhyaksha, worked by slaves, hired labour and settled cultivators. The deportation of 150,000 from Kalinga is often read as forced settlement of cleared land.
  • State monopolies: mining (the Arthashastra calls mines the source of the treasury), salt, liquor, arms, forest produce, shipping. Guilds (shrenis) organised craft production and credit, while the state fixed prices, checked weights and levied tolls (sulka).
  • Punch-marked coins — silver karshapana (or pana) and copper, stamped with symbols such as crescent-on-hill and tree-in-railing. Being uninscribed, no coin bears Ashoka's name. The Arthashastra names the lakshanadhyaksha (mint) and rupadarshaka (coin examiner).
  • Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) marks the period's urban prosperity in the Ganga plain.

I. Mauryan Art — Court Art and Popular Art

Mauryan remains divide into court art (polished pillars, animal capitals, rock-cut caves) and popular art (yakshas and yakshis, terracottas, ringstones). The signature is the Mauryan polish — a mirror lustre on hard stone that later periods never matched.

Trap: only the core is Mauryan; the stone encasing and balustrade are Shunga, and the four carved toranas are later still — a "Mauryan gateway at Sanchi" is always wrong. Sanchi also has an Ashokan pillar with a lion capital bearing the Schism Edict. Mahinda and Sanghamitta, who took Buddhism to Sri Lanka, set out from Pataliputra in Bihar.

J. Decline — Successors and Competing Explanations

The Vishnu Purana names seven rulers after Ashoka — Suyasas, Dasharatha, Sangata, Salisuka, Somasarman, Satadhanvan and Brihadratha — covering 232 to 185 BCE. Other traditions insert Kunala and Samprati (a great patron in Jain memory), and several historians think the empire split between Pataliputra and Ujjain. In c. 185 BCE the last Maurya, Brihadratha, was killed by his commander-in-chief Pushyamitra Shunga — per Bana's Harshacharita, during a review of the army.

🔶 Bihar Connection
Bihar was not a region of the Mauryan Empire — it was its centre of gravity
  • Pataliputra (Patna district) — the imperial capital. Begun as a fort by Udayin at the Ganga–Son confluence. Megasthenes describes a parallelogram about 80 stadia by 15, defended by a timber palisade with 64 gates and 570 towers and a wide moat.
  • Bulandi Bagh (Patna) — the palisade found. Excavation uncovered parallel rows of upright sal beams preserved in waterlogged soil, matching Megasthenes almost exactly.
  • Kumrahar (Patna) — the 80-pillared hall. Excavated by D. B. Spooner in 1912–15 with funding from Sir Ratan Tata: a hall of highly polished sandstone pillars in rows of eight, identified with the Mauryan assembly or audience hall, plus wooden platforms and NBPW pottery.
  • Barabar caves (Jehanabad district) — India's oldest surviving rock-cut caves. Cut into hard granite and polished to a mirror finish inside, the Sudama, Karna Chaupar and Vishwakarma caves carry Ashokan dedications to the Ajivikas. Adjacent Lomas Rishi has the famous chaitya-arch façade but no Ashokan inscription.
  • Nagarjuni caves (Jehanabad district). Gopika, Vadathika and Vapiyaka, granted by Dasharatha, Ashoka's grandson, again to the Ajivikas — the patronage outlived Ashoka.
  • Sasaram (Rohtas district) — Minor Rock Edict. The only Ashokan rock edict in Bihar, from the group in which he speaks of his years as a lay follower. Rock edict in Bihar means Sasaram; pillar edicts mean Champaran.
  • Lauriya Nandangarh and Lauriya Areraj (West Champaran district). Two of the finest Ashokan columns, still standing where raised. Nandangarh carries a seated lion capital and Pillar Edicts I–VI; Areraj's shaft carries the edicts but has lost its capital.
  • Rampurva (West Champaran district). Two pillars — one with a bull capital of exceptional naturalism, now in Rashtrapati Bhavan; the other with a lion capital.
  • Kolhua, Vaishali (Vaishali district). A polished monolithic column with a lion capital; it bears no edict text.
  • Didarganj Yakshi (Patna). A life-size female chauri-bearer in polished sandstone found on the Ganga bank in 1917 — the showpiece of Mauryan popular art, though several art historians date it later. Now in the Bihar Museum, Patna.
  • Third Buddhist Council, Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE). Under Ashoka's patronage, presided over by Moggaliputta Tissa; credited with compiling the Kathavatthu and sending missions abroad, including Mahinda and Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka. Recorded in the Sri Lankan chronicles, not in Ashoka's inscriptions.
  • Bodh Gaya (Gaya district). The polished Vajrasana (Diamond Throne) slab beside the Bodhi tree is attributed to Ashoka's reign, and he is credited with the first shrine where the Mahabodhi Temple stands. Sanghamitta is said to have carried a Bodhi sapling from here to Anuradhapura.
  • Sonbhandar caves, Rajgir (Nalanda district). Two rock-cut chambers in the Vaibhara hill, popularly linked to a royal treasury and continuing the Barabar tradition; an inscription names a Jain teacher and most scholars date them well after the Mauryas — never assign them to Ashoka.

📰 Current Relevance
  • National Emblem: the Sarnath Lion Capital (four lions) is India's national emblem (adopted 26 January 1950). The Dhamma Chakra (wheel) appears on the national flag. Ashoka's legacy = India's official symbols.
  • Ashoka Chakra: the 24-spoke wheel on India's national flag — derived from the Sarnath Lion Capital. Represents dhamma (righteousness) and the continuity of the nation.
  • Dhamma as governance: Ashoka's principles of religious tolerance, welfare, non-violence, and ethical governance are cited as foundational to India's constitutional values.
  • Buddhism as world religion: Ashoka's missionaries (sent from Pataliputra, Bihar) spread Buddhism to Sri Lanka, SE Asia, Central Asia — making it a world religion with ~500 million followers today.
  • Pataliputra (Patna): one of the longest-inhabited cities in the world. The Mauryan capital is now the capital of Bihar. Archaeological excavations have revealed Mauryan-period wooden palisade walls.

✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Pillars at Delhi-Topra, Delhi-Meerut (transported by Firoz Shah Tughlaq), Lauriya-Araraj, Lauriya-Nandangarh, Rampurva (all Bihar), Sanchi, Sarnath, Lumbini, Rummindei. Several in Bihar!
Q14. Which Ashokan pillar is the national emblem of India?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — Sarnath Lion Capital (four lions) = India's national emblem (adopted 26 January 1950). Dhamma Chakra on the capital appears on the national flag.
Q15. Ashoka convened the Third Buddhist Council at which place?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Third Council: Pataliputra (Bihar, c. 250 BCE), Ashoka. Presided by Moggaliputta Tissa. Compiled Abhidhamma Pitaka. Sent missionaries abroad — Buddhism became world religion.
Q16. Ashoka sent his son and daughter as Buddhist missionaries to which country?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Mahendra (son) + Sanghamitra (daughter) to Sri Lanka. Sanghamitra carried a sapling of the Bodhi tree from Bodh Gaya (Bihar) to Anuradhapura. Theravada Buddhism established.
Q17. The Mauryan Empire was divided into how many provinces for administration?
✅ Correct Answer: (D) — 5 provinces: Uttarapatha (Taxila, North), Dakshinapatha (Suvarnagiri, South), Avantiratha (Ujjain, West), Prachyapatha (Tosali, East), Magadha (Pataliputra, central). Governed by Kumar/Aryaputra (royal prince).
Q18. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft and economics, was written by:
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Kautilya (Chanakya). Covers statecraft, economics, military, law, administration. Describes Mauryan admin (18 departments/tirthas). Rediscovered in 1909 by R. Shamasastry.
Q19. Megasthenes' work 'Indica' describes which Mauryan city in detail?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Megasthenes' 'Indica' describes Pataliputra: 9 mi × 1.5 mi, wooden wall (570 towers, 64 gates). Royal palace magnificent (Persian-influenced). City council of 30 (6 boards of 5). Its administration, city plan, society, the royal palace.
Q20. Chandragupta Maurya in his later years embraced which religion and where did he die?
✅ Correct Answer: (C) — Chandragupta embraced Jainism (influenced by Bhadrabahu). Abdicated for son Bindusara, became monk, went to Shravanabelagola (Karnataka). Performed Sallekhana (ritual fasting) ~297 BCE.
Q21. The Mauryan Empire declined after Ashoka's death. The last Mauryan emperor was killed by whom?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Last Maurya Brihadratha killed by general Pushyamitra Shunga (c. 185 BCE) → Shunga dynasty. Empire lasted ~137 years (321-185 BCE). Causes: weak successors, decentralization, Brahminical reaction.
Q22. Ashoka's Dhamma (Dharma) was a secular code of conduct. Which of the following is NOT a principle of Ashoka's Dhamma?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Animal sacrifice is NOT a principle — Ashoka PROHIBITED it. Dhamma = ahimsa, tolerance, respect for parents/teachers/Brahmins, welfare, moderation, truthfulness, compassion.
Q23. Which of the following is NOT a major Ashokan inscription site in Bihar?
✅ Correct Answer: (B) — Sanchi is in Madhya Pradesh, NOT Bihar. Bihar sites: Lauriya-Araraj, Lauriya-Nandangarh, Rampurva (all West Champaran), Bodh Gaya, Rajgir, Barabar caves.
Q24. The Barabar Caves (near Gaya, Bihar) were donated by Ashoka to which religious sect?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Barabar Caves (near Gaya, Bihar) — oldest rock-cut caves in India — donated to Ajivikas (founded by Makkhali Gosala). Ajivikas believed in niyati (destiny). Dasharatha (Ashoka's grandson) also donated at Nagarjuna hills.
Q25. Which Ashokan edict specifically mentions the Kalinga War and his remorse?
✅ Correct Answer: (A) — Rock Edict XIII describes Kalinga War (261 BCE): '150,000 deported, 100,000 killed, many more died.' Ashoka expressed remorse, resolved to practice Dhamma-vijaya (moral conquest) instead of war.
