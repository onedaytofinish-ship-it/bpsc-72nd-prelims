A. What "early medieval" means — and why historians argue about it

The early medieval period runs roughly from the death of Harsha (647 CE) to the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate (1206 CE). It is the least-loved stretch of Indian history and the most misunderstood: older textbooks dismissed it as a "dark age" of political fragmentation between the Guptas and the Turks. That verdict has been comprehensively revised, and BPSC has begun to test the revision rather than the old cliché.

InterpretationAssociated historianCore claim
Indian FeudalismR.S. SharmaLand grants to brahmins and officials fragmented royal authority, decayed trade, coinage and towns, and tied peasants to the soil — an Indian parallel to European feudalism
Segmentary StateBurton Stein (on the Cholas)The king's ritual sovereignty was wide but his actual administrative control shrank with distance; real power sat with locality assemblies
Integrative PolityHermann Kulke, B.D. ChattopadhyayaNot decline but expansion — new regional states, new agrarian frontiers, a third phase of urbanisation, and local clans absorbed into a wider order

The pragmatic exam position: the period saw genuine political fragmentation but also genuine expansion — of agriculture into forested tracts, of regional languages and scripts, of temple-centred economies, and of the local elites who became "Rajputs".

B. The Tripartite Struggle for Kanauj (c. 750–950 CE)

For two centuries three powers fought over Kanauj — not for its wealth alone, but because Harsha had ruled from there and holding it carried the prestige of paramountcy over the Ganga plain. This three-cornered contest is the organising fact of eighth- and ninth-century north India.

PowerBaseKey rulersWhat they are remembered for
Gurjara-PratiharasMalwa & RajasthanNagabhata I, Vatsaraja, Nagabhata II, Mihira Bhoja (836–885), Mahendrapala IEventually held Kanauj; Nagabhata I checked the Arab advance from Sind — the "bulwark" reputation. The Arab traveller Al-Masudi noted their large cavalry
PalasBengal & BiharGopala, Dharmapala (770–810), Devapala (810–850)Buddhist patrons; Dharmapala installed his own nominee at Kanauj at the height of Pala power
RashtrakutasDeccan (Manyakheta)Dantidurga (founder, c. 753), Krishna I, Govinda III, Amoghavarsha I, Krishna IIIRepeatedly raided north and broke both rivals; Krishna I built the rock-cut Kailasa temple at Ellora; Amoghavarsha wrote Kavirajamarga, the earliest Kannada literary work

Outcome: the Pratiharas won Kanauj but were exhausted by it. The Rashtrakuta raid under Indra III (916 CE) sacked Kanauj and broke Pratihara power for good; their feudatories — Chandelas, Paramaras, Chauhans, Tomaras — became independent kingdoms. The fragmentation the Turks later exploited was manufactured here.

🧠 Mnemonic — "PPR fought for K": Pratihara (west) · Pala (east) · Rashtrakuta (south) all fought for Kanauj. The Pratiharas won the city; the Rashtrakutas won the wars; the Palas won the culture.

C. Where did the Rajputs come from? Four theories

"Rajput" (from rajaputra, king's son) appears as a political identity only from about the ninth century. Its origin is a genuine historical controversy, and BPSC has asked it as a straight matching question.

TheoryProposed byArgumentProblem with it
Agnikula (fire-born)Prithviraj Raso, Chand BardaiFour clans — Pratihara, Chauhan, Solanki, Parmar — sprang from a sacrificial fire pit at Mount AbuA bardic legend from the 12th century, not evidence
Foreign originCol. James Tod, V.A. SmithDescendants of Scythians, Hunas and other Central Asian groups absorbed after the fifth centuryColonial-era reasoning; overstates foreign descent
Indigenous KshatriyaG.H. Ojha, C.V. VaidyaPure descent from Vedic Kshatriya lineages, solar and lunarReads later genealogies back into the past
Social process ("Rajputisation")B.D. ChattopadhyayaLocal chiefs and tribal elites acquired land and power, then commissioned genealogies linking themselves to solar/lunar linesNow the mainstream scholarly view

The three claimed lineages — Suryavansha (solar: Sisodias of Mewar), Chandravansha (lunar: Rathores of Marwar), and Agnivansha (fire: the four Agnikula clans).

Chittorgarh Fort, the largest fort in India and seat of the Sisodias of Mewar. Three jauhars are associated with it — 1303 (Alauddin Khalji), 1535 (Bahadur Shah of Gujarat) and 1568 (Akbar). Note the chronology trap: Chittor's fame belongs to the later medieval period, while the Rajput dynasties that mattered in the early medieval centuries were the Pratiharas, Chauhans, Paramaras and Chandelas.

E. The Turkish conquest — and why the Rajputs lost

BattleYearCombatantsResult
First Battle of Tarain1191Prithviraj III vs Muhammad GhoriRajput victory; Prithviraj released Ghori instead of pursuing him
Second Battle of Tarain1192Prithviraj III vs Muhammad GhoriGhori's victory; Prithviraj captured and killed — the decisive battle for north India
Battle of Chandawar1194Jayachandra Gahadavala vs GhoriGhori's victory; Kanauj and the doab fall, ending organised Rajput resistance

Why the Rajputs lost is a favourite short-note question. It was not a failure of courage:

  • No unified command. Rajput armies were coalitions of feudal levies under proud, mutually jealous chiefs; the Turks fought under one general with one plan.
  • Wrong arm of service. Rajputs relied on elephants and heavy, slow formations; Ghori's mounted archers used speed, feigned retreat and encirclement — mobility beat mass.
  • Defensive strategic outlook. Rajputs fought to repel invasions, never to conquer the invader's base. Ghori returned from defeat in 1191; Prithviraj could not return the favour.
  • A code that cost battles. Releasing a defeated enemy was honourable and catastrophic. In 1192 Ghori attacked at dawn, against convention, while the Rajputs were still forming up.
  • Social rigidity. Fighting was a caste monopoly, which capped the manpower an Indian kingdom could raise relative to its population.
PYQ ALERT The chronology trap. BPSC repeatedly asks candidates to order Tarain I (1191), Tarain II (1192) and Chandawar (1194), or to pair a dynasty with its monument. The classic wrong pairings offered are Khajuraho with the Paramaras (it is Chandela), Modhera with the Chandelas (it is Solanki), and the Kailasa temple at Ellora with the Cholas (it is Rashtrakuta, under Krishna I).

F. The Cholas — political history

While north India fragmented, the far south produced the most durable and best-documented state of the era. The Cholas of Thanjavur (850–1279) left thousands of inscriptions, which is why their administration is known in more detail than any other Indian polity before the Mughals.

RulerReignAchievement
Vijayalayac. 850Founder; captured Thanjavur from the Muttaraiyar and built the Nishumbhasudini temple
Aditya I871–907Ended Pallava power
Parantaka I907–955Issued the Uttaramerur inscriptions; defeated at Takkolam (949) by Rashtrakuta Krishna III
Rajaraja I985–1014Greatest administrator; built the Brihadisvara (Rajarajeshwara) temple at Thanjavur, completed 1010; conquered northern Sri Lanka and the Maldives; ordered the land survey that underpins Chola revenue
Rajendra I1014–1044Greatest conqueror; marched to the Ganga and took the title Gangaikonda Chola, founding a new capital Gangaikondacholapuram; launched the naval expedition against Srivijaya (1025)
Kulottunga I1070–1122United the Chalukya and Chola lines; abolished tolls, earning the title Sungam Tavirtta ("he who abolished tolls")
Decline: weakened by Hoysala and Pandya pressure; extinguished by the Pandyas under Maravarman Kulasekhara in 1279.

G. Chola administration — the part BPSC actually asks about

Chola government worked on two levels that barely touched: an imperial superstructure of king, court and army, and beneath it a dense layer of self-governing localities that collected their own revenue, ran their own irrigation and judged their own disputes. It is this village autonomy — not the conquests — that makes the Cholas exam-critical.

The Uttaramerur inscriptions (Parantaka I, 919 and 921 CE, Kanchipuram district) record the constitution of a village sabha in extraordinary detail — the single most quoted evidence for local self-government in Indian history:

  • The village was divided into thirty wards (kudumbu), each nominating candidates.
  • Members were chosen by kudavolai — names written on palm-leaf tickets, placed in a pot, drawn out by a young boy. Election by lot, not by vote.
  • Qualifications: ownership of at least a quarter veli of taxable land; a house on one's own site; age between 35 and 70; knowledge of the Vedas and the mantras.
  • Disqualifications: anyone who had failed to submit accounts of a previous term, and his close relatives; those guilty of the major sins; those who had eaten forbidden food.
  • Work was done through variyams (committees) — samvatsara-variyam (annual), eri-variyam (tanks and irrigation), totta-variyam (gardens), pon-variyam (gold), nyaya-variyam (justice).
🧠 Mnemonic — "U-S-N" for who meets where: Ur = Usual village · Sabha = Sacred (brahmadeya) village · Nagaram = Negotiants, i.e. merchants. And remember kudavolai = pot + ticket — chosen by lot, which is why calling it "democratic election" in the modern sense is the trap.

The temple was the economic engine, not merely a place of worship: it was the largest landowner, employer and creditor in its locality, lending at interest, storing grain, and paying priests, dancers, musicians, cooks and accountants. Long-distance trade was handled by powerful merchant guilds — the Ayyavole (the "Five Hundred" of Aihole), the Manigramam and the Anjuvannam (largely West Asian traders). Chola embassies reached Song China in 1015, 1033 and 1077.

I. Chola navy, overseas expeditions and art

  • The strongest navy in Indian history before the modern era. Rajendra I's fleet crossed the Bay of Bengal in 1025 to attack Srivijaya (Sumatra/Malaya) — the only sustained overseas offensive ever mounted by an Indian state — to force open the trade route to China.
  • Northern Sri Lanka was annexed and administered as a Chola province; the Maldives were taken under Rajaraja I.
  • Dravida temple architecture matured here: garbhagriha (sanctum) under a towering vimana, fronted by mandapas, enclosed by a walled courtyard with a gopuram gateway. In Chola temples the vimana dominates — the giant gopurams came later, under the Pandyas and Nayakas.
  • The Brihadisvara temple (1010) carries a vimana of about 66 m, the tallest of its day; UNESCO listed it in 1987 as part of the Great Living Chola Temples.
  • Chola bronzes, cast by the cire perdue (lost-wax) process, are the period's supreme artistic achievement — above all the Nataraja, Shiva as lord of the cosmic dance.
Brihadisvara Temple, Thanjavur — begun by Rajaraja I and completed in 1010 CE, dedicated to Shiva. Note that the vimana over the sanctum, not the gateway, is the tallest structure: this is the signature of Chola-period Dravida architecture and the way to distinguish it from later Pandya and Nayaka temples, where the gopurams tower over everything.

K. Society, economy and culture

  • Agrarian expansion. Land grants pushed cultivation into forest and tribal tracts; brahmin settlements carried plough agriculture, Sanskrit and temple ritual into new regions, absorbing tribal groups as jatis.
  • Caste proliferation. The four varnas splintered into hundreds of occupational jatis. Scribal groups such as the Kayasthas emerged as a distinct caste from the administrative demand for record-keepers.
  • Women's position declined relative to the earlier period — earlier marriage, restricted property rights, and the first widespread epigraphic references to sati among ruling families. Temple devadasis were an institution of the era.
  • Regional languages arrive. Kannada (Amoghavarsha's Kavirajamarga), Tamil devotional literature, and early Bengali Charyapadas composed under Pala patronage.
  • Temple architecture crystallises into the two great styles — Nagara in the north (Khajuraho, Modhera, Lingaraja, Konark) and Dravida in the south (Thanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram) — with Vesara as the Deccan hybrid.
PYQ ALERT Uttaramerur is the highest-value single fact in this chapter. It is the standard evidence cited for local self-government in India, and questions attach to it from three directions: which ruler (Parantaka I), which method (kudavolai — selection by lot from a pot), and which qualification (land ownership, age 35–70, Vedic knowledge). A question asking which was not a qualification is the most common form.

🔶 Bihar Connection
🟠 The Palas — Bihar's last great imperial dynasty (750–1161 CE)
  • Gopala's election (c. 750). After Shashanka's death Bengal and Bihar collapsed into a century of anarchy the sources call matsyanyaya — "the law of the fishes", where the big devour the small. The chiefs ended it by electing Gopala king. Indian tradition remembers this as its first elected monarch, and BPSC has asked it directly.
  • Dharmapala (c. 770–810) made the Palas an imperial power, intervened at Kanauj in the tripartite struggle, and founded Vikramashila (near modern Bhagalpur) and Odantapuri (near Bihar Sharif) — with Nalanda, the three great monastic universities of Bihar.
  • Devapala (c. 810–850) extended Pala power to its widest, receiving an embassy from the Sailendra king of Java, who endowed a monastery at Nalanda.
  • Vikramashila specialised in Tantric Buddhism and was governed by a board of scholars under a head — the most famous being Atisha Dipankara Srijnana, who left for Tibet in 1042 and reshaped Tibetan Buddhism. Bihar's intellectual export to the Himalaya runs through this institution.
  • The Pala school of art — bronze and black-stone sculpture from Nalanda and Kurkihar, and illustrated palm-leaf manuscripts — travelled with the monks and became a foundation of Tibetan and Southeast Asian Buddhist art.
  • Later Palas: Mahipala I revived the dynasty in the 11th century; Ramapala, the last significant ruler, is the subject of Sandhyakara Nandi's Ramacharitam.
  • End of the line. The Palas gave way to the Senas (Vijayasena, Ballalasena, Lakshmanasena), a Hindu dynasty under whom Buddhist patronage dried up. Around 1200–1203 Bakhtiyar Khalji stormed Bihar, destroying Nalanda, Vikramashila and Odantapuri — the event that ends both Pala Bihar and organised Indian Buddhism.
  • Why this matters for the 72nd: the Palas are the strongest Bihar-specific content in the entire medieval syllabus. Expect Gopala's election, Dharmapala and Vikramashila, and Bakhtiyar Khalji's destruction of the monasteries.
Nalanda Mahavihara, Bihar. Founded far earlier under the Guptas (Kumaragupta I), Nalanda was sustained through the early medieval centuries by Pala patronage alongside their own foundations at Vikramashila and Odantapuri. Watch the trap: Nalanda was not founded by the Palas — they were its patrons, not its founders. UNESCO listed the site in 2016.
