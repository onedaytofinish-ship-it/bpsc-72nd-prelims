Read this as one chain. Vijayanagara (1336–1646) and the Bahmani kingdom (1347–c.1527) were born eleven years apart out of the collapse of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's Deccan authority and fought for two centuries over the Raichur doab. The Bahmani state split into five Deccan Sultanates, four of which destroyed Vijayanagara at Talikota (1565). Those sultanates employed Maratha cavalry captains — and from that class Shivaji built a kingdom.

A. Vijayanagara — Foundation and the Vidyaranya Tradition

Founded in 1336 on the Tungabhadra by Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, sons of Sangama. Tradition says the brothers were Kakatiya officers taken to Delhi and converted, sent back to hold Kampili for Tughlaq, then reconverted and blessed by the sage Vidyaranya of Sringeri — hence the capital's alternative name Vidyanagara. Modern scholarship treats the conversion story as later legend; learn the traditional version with that caution. The tutelary deity was Virupaksha, and royal orders were signed Sri Virupaksha.

Virupaksha temple, Hampi (Vijayanagara district, Karnataka) — UNESCO-inscribed in 1986, and on the List of World Heritage in Danger from 1999 to 2006. Traps: Hampi stands on the Tungabhadra, not the Krishna or Kaveri; and Virupaksha is a pre-Vijayanagara shrine enlarged by the empire, so do not date it to 1336.

D. Talikota, 23 January 1565

By the 1550s power lay with Aliya Rama Raya of the Aravidu family, ruling for the Tuluva puppet Sadashiva Raya. His habit of hiring Vijayanagara's weight out to one sultanate against another drove them into a coalition. The field lay near Rakkasagi and Tangadagi on the Krishna — hence the accurate name Rakshasi-Tangadi.

  • The coalition: Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda and Bidar. Berar took no part. Many keys loosely write "the five Deccan Sultanates" — know the accurate four, but treat a collective option as the intended answer.
  • The turn: Vijayanagara was winning early; the defection of two of its own Muslim commanders and the beheading of Rama Raya broke the line.
  • Aftermath: the capital was plundered over months and never reoccupied — hence ruins today.
  • What it did not do: end the empire in law. Tirumala Deva Raya founded the Aravidu line at Penukonda; the dynasty ran to 1646.
Exam Trap Date pairs examiners swap: Vijayanagara 1336 versus Bahmani 1347 — Vijayanagara is the earlier foundation. Talikota 1565 versus Vijayanagara's formal end 1646. Bidar becomes capital 1425 versus Mahmud Gawan's execution 1481.

G. Hampi — The Remains

  • Vitthala temple: the finest complex, with the stone chariot — in fact a Garuda shrine carved as a temple car — and the "musical" pillars. The chariot appears on the ₹50 note.
  • Virupaksha temple: still in worship, the one living monument among the ruins.
  • Secular remains: Lotus Mahal, elephant stables, Queen's Bath — Indo-Islamic arches and domes, proof of borrowing from Deccani architecture.
  • Ritual centre: the Mahanavami Dibba, from which the ruler watched the Dasara review Paes described; the Hazara Rama temple with its Ramayana friezes.

H. The Bahmani Kingdom, 1347

Created by the revolt of the Amiran-i-Sadah, the Deccan-posted "centurions", against Muhammad bin Tughlaq. Their leader took the throne in 1347 as Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah, popularly Hasan Gangu, with Gulbarga (Ahsanabad) as capital. In 1425 Ahmad Shah Wali I moved it to Bidar, which became the cultural capital of the Deccan and gave its name to bidri inlay work. Firuz Shah Bahmani (1397–1422) was the most learned of the line.

I. Mahmud Gawan — Reforms and the Faction Fight

HeadContent

The structural cause of collapse was the split between the Deccanis — settled Indian Muslims with the Habshi (Abyssinian) element — and the Afaqis or Gharbians, newcomers from Persia, Turkey and Arabia who took the best posts and were usually Shia. Gawan was an Afaqi; his execution was the Deccani party's victory and removed the last competent central authority. Within a decade the governors were independent.

K. Golconda Diamonds and Deccani Architecture

  • Diamonds: Golconda was the cutting and trading emporium; the stones came mainly from the Kollur and allied mines in the Krishna valley. Koh-i-Noor, Hope and Darya-i-Noor all pass through Golconda in the trade records, alongside Coromandel textiles shipped from Masulipatnam.
  • Charminar: built in 1591 by Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah at the founding of Hyderabad — four minarets on a square base.
  • Gol Gumbaz, Bijapur: tomb of Muhammad Adil Shah, completed 1656, its dome carried on interlocking arches without external buttressing; the gallery inside the drum is the whispering gallery. Largest masonry dome in India.
  • Ibrahim Rauza, Bijapur: tomb and mosque for Ibrahim Adil Shah II, who wrote the Kitab-i-Nauras, invoking Saraswati as well as the Prophet.

L. The Marathas — Preconditions

Four conditions made a Maratha state possible. Geography: the Sahyadris and the Maval uplands made hill forts decisive and heavy cavalry useless. Bhakti: Jnaneshwar, Namdev, Eknath and above all Tukaram (1608–1650) of the Varkari tradition centred on Vitthal of Pandharpur gave a Marathi devotional culture cutting across caste, alongside Ramdas (1608–1681) of the Dasbodh, whom later tradition made Shivaji's guide. Service: the Bhonsles, Mores and Ghorpades had served as cavalry commanders and deshmukhs under Ahmadnagar and Bijapur, with Malik Ambar's guerrilla campaigns as their school. Opportunity: the fall of Ahmadnagar (1636) left a vacuum when Shivaji came of age.

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj (1630–1680), crowned at Raigad on 6 June 1674 by Gaga Bhatt of Varanasi. Traps: his title was Chhatrapati, never "Peshwa" — the Peshwa was his prime minister and became effective ruler only two generations later; and under the Treaty of Purandar it was his son Sambhaji who took a Mughal mansab.

  • All except the Nyayadhish and Panditrao were expected to command troops — an executive council, not a body of scholars.
  • Offices were appointive and transferable under Shivaji; they became hereditary later, which is how the Peshwa's office turned into a dynasty.
  • He largely avoided jagirs, preferring cash salaries from revenue assessed with the kathi rod inherited from Malik Ambar's settlement.
  • Cavalry came as the bargir, horsed by the state, and the siledar, who brought his own mount. Bargir is the word behind the Bihari and Bengali bargi.

P. Successors and the Peshwas

  • Sambhaji (1680–89): sheltered Aurangzeb's rebel son Akbar; captured at Sangameshwar and executed in 1689. His death hardened the resistance.
  • Rajaram (1689–1700): moved the war south to Jinji, which held until 1698; the state survived as dispersed guerrilla warfare.
  • Tarabai (regent from 1700): Rajaram's widow directed the war in her infant son's name and pushed raids into Mughal Gujarat and Malwa.
  • Shahu (from 1707): Sambhaji's son, released in the succession struggle after Aurangzeb's death; defeated Tarabai at Khed. The Treaty of Warna (1731) fixed the split — Shahu at Satara, Tarabai's line at Kolhapur.
  • Balaji Vishwanath (Peshwa 1713–20): in 1719, through the Sayyid brothers, secured Mughal recognition of swaraj plus the right to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi across the six Deccan subahs.
  • Baji Rao I (1720–40): author of the northward drive. Beat the Nizam at Palkhed (1728) and near Bhopal (1737–38, yielding Malwa), raided the outskirts of Delhi in 1737, and through his brother Chimaji Appa took Bassein from the Portuguese in 1739. Died 1740, aged thirty-nine.
  • Balaji Baji Rao, Nana Saheb (1740–61): maximum extent — Attock and Peshawar briefly held in 1758 — and maximum overstretch, ending at Panipat.

The confederacy's looseness was a strength while expanding and a defect against one disciplined opponent. The English broke it house by house across three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–82, Treaty of Salbai; 1803–05; 1817–18); in 1818 the Peshwa's office was abolished.

🔶 Bihar Connection
A Peninsular Topic with Eastern Consequences — Handle Precisely
  • Start from the honest position. Neither Vijayanagara nor the Bahmani kingdom nor any Deccan Sultanate ever held an acre in present-day Bihar, and the Maratha state never annexed Bihar. That absence is the trap: when a question pairs a Deccan term — amara-nayaka, ayagar, Ashta Pradhan — with an eastern site such as Patna, Gaya or Bhagalpur, the pairing is almost always wrong.
  • What did reach Bihar: the bargi raids, 1740s–50s. The Nagpur Bhonsles under Raghuji Bhonsle, with Bhaskar Pandit (Bhaskar Ram Kolhatkar) as field commander, raided Bengal, Bihar and Odisha from 1741. Bargi comes from bargir, the state-horsed trooper, and survives in folk memory as a byword for terror. Bhaskar Pandit was killed in 1744 when Alivardi Khan trapped him at a negotiation. These were raids for tribute and plunder — not conquest or administration.
  • Alivardi Khan's settlement of 1751. The Nawab of Bengal, whose subah then included Bihar, bought peace by ceding Odisha (Cuttack) to the Nagpur Bhonsles and paying an annual chauth of about twelve lakh rupees. Note the geography: the Marathas took Odisha, not Bihar.
  • The Panipat–Buxar link. The defeat at Panipat in 1761 removed the only Indian power able to contest north India as a whole. Three years later the English beat Shuja-ud-Daula, Mir Qasim and Shah Alam II at Buxar (22 October 1764, Buxar district) and in 1765 took the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. No Maratha army fought at Buxar: write "Maratha collapse cleared the field".
  • Ahilyabai Holkar at Gaya. The Holkar queen of Indore (1767–1795) financed temple building across the subcontinent, and the present Vishnupad temple at Gaya (Gaya district) was rebuilt under her patronage in 1787 — the one standing Maratha mark on Bihar, and patronage rather than political control.
  • Parallel state formation, fourteenth century. The weakening of the Tughlaq centre that produced the Bahmani revolt of 1347 also produced independent Bengal under the Ilyas Shahis and, from 1394, the Sharqi sultanate of Jaunpur. Bihar sat as a frontier between Delhi, Jaunpur and Bengal — the eastern counterpart of Deccan fragmentation.
  • Sufi parallels. Gulbarga became the seat of the Chishti saint Gesu Daraz under Bahmani patronage; Bihar's own fourteenth-century centre was the Firdausi silsila at Bihar Sharif (Nalanda district) under Sharfuddin Yahya Maneri.
  • Vocabulary that misleads. Deshmukh, mokasa and sardeshmukhi have no counterpart in Bihar, where the operative terms are zamindar, ijaradar and the Permanent Settlement of 1793.
