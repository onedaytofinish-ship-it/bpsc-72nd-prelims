A. Dating the Vedic Age — and Why Scholars Disagree EXAM CORE

The bracket is c. 1500–600 BCE: Early (Rig Vedic) c. 1500–1000 BCE, Later Vedic c. 1000–600 BCE. Older datings varied wildly — Max Müller proposed c. 1200 BCE, B. G. Tilak (The Orion) c. 4500 BCE on astronomical grounds. The Aryan question stays contested: the 2019 Rakhigarhi ancient-DNA study found no steppe ancestry in the Harappan sampled, while genome-wide work places steppe ancestry entering South Asia c. 2000–1500 BCE. Arya means "noble" — a self-description, not a race.

B. The Four Layers of Vedic Literature

Every Veda has four successive strata, and the sequence is a favourite ordering question. All four are shruti ("heard", revealed); Vedangas, epics, Puranas and Dharmashastras are smriti. These are not four books but four ages of thought on one text.

A recurring trap: older texts speak of trayi vidya, the "threefold knowledge" of Rig, Sama and Yajur only — "the Vedas were always four" is wrong for the earliest period.

Rigveda manuscript: 1,028 hymns in 10 mandalas, transmitted orally for millennia before being written. Trap: the precise claim is "oldest text in any Indo-European language", and this manuscript is medieval though the composition is Early Vedic — never confuse a text's date with its manuscript's.

D. Brahmanas, Aranyakas, Upanishads — How They Differ

The Brahmanas are ritual encyclopaedias; the Aranyakas the hinge; the Upanishads the revolt. In the Brahmana period the priest claims the yajna itself compels the gods. The Aranyakas, for forest hermits who could not mount a public sacrifice, let the rite be meditated rather than performed. The Upanishads finish the argument: if the rite works through knowledge, knowledge alone suffices.

Both were composed and expanded from roughly the 5th/4th century BCE to the 4th century CE. The debate matters because the epics keep a Later Vedic memory at their core while the texts are layered over eight hundred years. For the examination, the epics are sources about the Later Vedic age, not products of it.

G. Geography — Sapta Sindhava to the Middle Ganga

The Rig Veda's world is Sapta Sindhava, "land of seven rivers" — Punjab, eastern Afghanistan and the Sarasvati basin. The Ganga is named once (Nadistuti sukta, Mandala X), the Yamuna thrice, and the Sarasvati is naditama, "best of rivers". The Later Vedic centre shifts east to Kuru–Panchala, then into Kosala, Kashi and Videha.

H. The Battle of Ten Kings (Dasarajna)

Recorded in Mandala VII and fought on the Parushni (Ravi), the dasarajna pitted Sudas of the Bharata tribe against ten tribes — the five Panchajana (Puru, Yadu, Turvasha, Anu, Druhyu) plus the Alina, Paktha, Bhalanas, Shiva and Vishanin. Sudas won with his priest Vasishtha, having dismissed Vishvamitra, who joined the rivals. What followed matters more: the Bharatas later merged with the Purus to form the Kurus, and the country is named Bharatavarsha after this tribe.

The ratnins or "jewel-bearers" were the twelve functionaries at whose houses the king made offerings during his consecration — the queen, purohita, senani, charioteer, village headman and others. Authority was still negotiated with leading households even as sacrifice inflated his claims.

Iron is the engine of the transition. R. S. Sharma argued that iron alone could clear the damp eastern forest and turn its stiff soil, yielding the surplus that fed priests, specialists and a ruling class — hence the janapada. The counter is that the earliest iron finds are mostly weapons, so iron may have mattered first for conquest.

K. Society — Varna, Gotra, Ashrama, Purushartha

Varna means "colour"; in the Rig Veda it separates arya varna from dasa varna, and the fourfold order appears only in the late Purusha Sukta (X.90). Early Vedic divisions were occupational and permeable — one hymn has a speaker whose father is a physician and mother a grinder of corn. The Later Vedic Brahmanas make the order hereditary: only the first three varnas are dvija, entitled to upanayana.

  • The ashrama scheme is prescriptive — an ideal for dvija males, not a record of how people lived.
  • Later Vedic texts name only three ashramas clearly; sannyasa is fixed only at the close of the period.

L. Women — A Decline That Was Real but Not Uniform

  • Early Vedic: women composed hymns — Lopamudra, Ghosha, Apala, Vishvavara are named brahmavadinis in the Rig Veda.
  • They attended the vidatha and sabha, underwent upanayana and joined their husbands in sacrifice; there was no sati, no purdah, no child marriage, and widow remarriage was allowed.
  • Later Vedic: exclusion from the sabha; the Maitrayani Samhita groups women with dice and liquor as evils; the Aitareya Brahmana calls a daughter a source of misery, a son the light of heaven.
  • Women also lost property rights, upanayana and Vedic study, and the marriage age fell.
  • The nuance BPSC exploits: the two most famous women philosophers, Gargi and Maitreyi, are Later Vedic, from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad; only Lopamudra is Early Vedic. The decline hit women as a class without erasing exceptional individuals.

M. Religion — From Nature Gods to the Lord of Creatures

Rig Vedic religion is naturalistic and anthropomorphic: gods are personified natural forces praised for material boons. There are 33 gods in three groups of eleven — terrestrial (Agni, Soma, Prithvi), atmospheric (Indra, Rudra, Vayu, Maruts) and celestial (Dyaus, Varuna, Mitra, Surya, Vishnu, Ushas). There were no temples and no images; worship meant offering into fire.

Governing ideaRta — cosmic and moral order upheld by VarunaRta yields to dharma, karma, samsara, moksha

N. The Upanishadic Reaction — Brahman, Atman, Karma, Moksha

By c. 800–600 BCE sacrifice had become ruinously expensive and socially exclusive, and a counter-current appears inside the Vedic corpus itself. The Mundaka Upanishad dismisses the great sacrifices as "unsteady boats"; the Chandogya satirises priestly chanting as a procession of dogs. In place of ritual the Upanishads offer jnana.

India's national motto Satyameva Jayate — "Truth alone triumphs" — comes from the Mundaka Upanishad (3.1.6) of the Atharva Veda. Adopted with the State Emblem on 26 January 1950, it is inscribed in Devanagari below the abacus of the Sarnath Lion Capital.

The Vedic corpus as one evolving library: Samhita → Brahmana → Aranyaka → Upanishad, that is hymn → ritual → symbol → philosophy. Trap: the Upanishads are part of the Vedas (shruti), not a separate later scripture, and not the same as the Puranas or epics.

🔶 Bihar Connection
Bihar — Where the Later Vedic Age Arrives, and Where It Turns Philosophical
  • 1. The Rig Veda already knows Bihar — and dislikes it. Kikata (Rig Veda III.53.14), whose chief is Pramaganda, lies outside the Vedic fold; later tradition, including Yaska's Nirukta, identifies Kikata with Magadha. This is the earliest notice of the land that became Bihar — and it places Bihar outside the Early Vedic world.
  • 2. The Atharva Veda confirms the hostility. A well-known charm sends fever away to the Angas and Magadhas; groups there were classed as vratyas, admitted only by the vratyastoma rite. Between Early and Later Vedic, Bihar moves from beyond the pale to the intellectual centre of the culture — one of the sharpest reversals in ancient Indian history.
  • 3. The Shatapatha Brahmana narrates the arrival — Videgha Mathava and the Sadanira. The Shatapatha Brahmana (I.4.1) tells how Videgha Mathava, with his priest Gotama Rahugana, carried Agni Vaishvanara in his mouth and moved eastwards, the fire burning across the land behind him, until it reached the Sadanira — identified with the Gandak — which it would not cross. Formerly, the text says, Brahmanas would not cross it because Agni had not burnt over it; "now, however, many Brahmanas dwell east of it." Mathava then settled the land, which is Videha. This passage literally narrates the Vedic arrival in north Bihar, and explains why the marshy forested plain had first to be cleared and made ritually fit. The Sadanira also marked the Kosala–Videha boundary.
  • 4. Videha and King Janaka — the philosopher-king of Mithila. Videha covers today's Darbhanga–Madhubani–Sitamarhi belt, its capital at Mithila. In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad Janaka is no passive host: he questions the sages himself and offers a thousand cows with gold on their horns to the most learned brahmana present. His court proves that Kshatriya rulers, not only Brahmanas, drove Upanishadic thought.
  • 5. Yajnavalkya — the giant of the Mithila tradition. He claimed Janaka's prize and defeated every challenger, including the king's hotri priest Ashvala. He first states the doctrine of karma — privately, to Jaratkarava Artabhaga — defines the Self as neti neti, and is credited with the Shukla Yajur Veda.
  • 6. Gargi Vachaknavi and Maitreyi — women in the debate. Gargi pressed Yajnavalkya on what the universe is "woven, warp and woof" upon, until he warned that her head would fall off if she questioned further. Maitreyi, one of his two wives, asked whether wealth could bring immortality and, told it could not, chose instruction over property. Both dialogues are in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, set in Videha — and both women are Later Vedic, from the period when women's status as a class was falling.
  • 7. Mithila as a permanent seat of learning. The region kept the reputation Janaka's court gave it: it became home to the Nyaya school of logic, to Mandana Mishra (whose debate with Shankaracharya is placed at Mahishi in Saharsa district), Vachaspati Mishra, Gangesha Upadhyaya and Vidyapati.
  • 8. How this sets up the Mahajanapadas. Later Vedic settlement of the middle Ganga plain is what makes the sixteen Mahajanapadas possible. Videha's monarchy gave way to a republic: it joined the Vajji (Vrijji) confederacy centred on Vaishali, with the Licchavis and Jnatrikas. Vajji and Magadha then fought for supremacy, Magadha won — and Bihar, marginal in the Rig Veda, became the political centre of India within two centuries.
BPSC FOCUS What BPSC asks here: Sadanira = Gandak; the text recording Videgha Mathava (Shatapatha Brahmana — not an Upanishad, the commonest distractor); the Yajnavalkya–Gargi debate (Brihadaranyaka, not Chandogya); Janaka's kingdom (Videha, not Vaishali, the later Vajji capital); and Kikata = Magadha. Expect a statement-pair on whether Bihar lay inside the Early Vedic horizon: the answer is no.
🔗 Cross-Topic Connections
  • Topic 98 — Indus Valley Civilisation: Harappans were urban, planned, literate, trading; Early Vedic people rural, pastoral, non-literate, chariot-borne. Mature Harappan ends c. 1900 BCE and the Rig Veda begins c. 1500 BCE, so "the Aryans destroyed the IVC" is not the accepted position; Bhagwanpura shows Late Harappan and PGW levels overlapping.
  • Topic 100 — Mahajanapadas: The Later Vedic janapada is the direct ancestor of the mahajanapada. Kuru and Panchala decline, Videha is absorbed into Vajji, and power shifts to the middle Ganga plain where iron ore, elephants and river routes let Magadha dominate. Second urbanisation, coinage and NBPW belong to that stage, not the Vedic age.
  • Topic 101 — Buddhism & Jainism: The heterodox systems inherit the Upanishadic vocabulary — karma, samsara, moksha/nirvana, renunciation — while rejecting Vedic authority, sacrificial violence and varna hierarchy. Both arise in the same eastern region that produced the Brihadaranyaka debates, from Kshatriya clans there (Shakyas; Jnatrikas of Kundagrama near Vaishali).
  • Topic 106 — Ancient Art & Literature: The Vedic corpus founds the Sanskrit tradition — the Vedangas produced Panini's Ashtadhyayi and Yaska's Nirukta, and the Sama Veda is the source of Indian classical music. Vedic religion left no architecture and no images, which is why Indian art history begins with the Mauryas — a frequent match-the-pair trap.
  • Topic 155 — Ancient Bihar: The opening chapter of Bihar's recorded history. Memorise the sequence: Kikata → Videgha Mathava crosses the Sadanira → Videha under Janaka → the Vajji confederacy at Vaishali → Magadha under Bimbisara and Ajatashatru → the Mauryas at Pataliputra.
