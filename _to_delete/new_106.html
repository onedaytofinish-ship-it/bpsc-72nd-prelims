Ancient art is examined as attribution: name the dynasty from the monument, the school from the Buddha image, the style from the tower. Learn diagnostic features, not name-lists.

Exam Pattern Four recurring question shapes: match monument to dynasty (Kailasa/Rashtrakuta, Barabar/Maurya); correctly-matched pairs (text and author, capital and site); assertion–reason on style, usually Gandhara's Greek debt; and odd-one-out, where a medieval item hides in an ancient list. Each needs patron, material and diagnostic feature.

2. Rock-cut Beginnings — Barabar

India's oldest surviving rock-cut caves are at Barabar (Jehanabad, Bihar), cut into granite and mirror-polished. Ashoka dedicated Sudama and Karan Chaupar to the Ajivikas; Dasharatha gave the three neighbouring Nagarjuni caves to the same sect. The Lomas Rishi facade copies a thatched hut in stone — ancestor of the horseshoe chaitya window.

8. Painting

Ajanta, Aurangabad district, Maharashtra; UNESCO 1983. The finer, later phase is under Vakataka patronage in the 5th century. Trap 1: not true frescoes — pigment was laid on dry lime-finished mud plaster mixed with rice husk and fibre, a tempera method. Trap 2: Ajanta is wholly Buddhist; Ellora adds Hindu and Jain caves. Trap 3: Padmapani and Vajrapani flank the shrine doorway of Cave 1.

Of about thirty excavations at Ajanta, significant painting survives in only four — the early halls Caves 9 and 10 and the Vakataka-age Caves 16 and 17 — besides fragments in Caves 1 and 2. Cave 16 holds the "Dying Princess"; Cave 17 is richest in Jataka narrative. Bagh (Dhar, MP) is Buddhist and technically close to Ajanta but far more secular; Sittanavasal (Pudukkottai, Tamil Nadu) is a Pandya-period Jain cave whose ceiling lotus-tank represents the samavasarana.

  • Mahabharata (Vyasa): traditionally grew from a core Jaya of about 8,800 verses, through a Bharata of 24,000, to the Shatasahasri Samhita of roughly 100,000 verses in 18 parvas; the Bhagavad Gita sits inside the Bhishma Parva.
  • Ramayana (Valmiki): about 24,000 verses in seven kandas, of which the first (Bala) and last (Uttara) are generally held to be later additions.
  • The point of the growth story: epics are stratified sources, evidence for the society that compiled them rather than for the events they narrate.

Trap Alert Six errors that cost marks: (1) Ajanta has Hindu caves — it does not; Ellora does. (2) Ashoka built Sanchi's gateways — they are Satavahana. (3) "Shikhara" means the same north and south — in the south it is only the crowning stone. (4) Kailasa is Chalukyan — it is Rashtrakuta. (5) Ajanta's murals are true frescoes — they are tempera on dry plaster. (6) The Mehrauli Iron Pillar is Mauryan — it is Gupta.

One-Line Discriminators

  • Mirror polish on the stone? Think Mauryan first.
  • Grey schist, wavy hair? Gandhara. Red spotted sandstone, bare right shoulder? Mathura.
  • Apsidal hall with a stupa inside? Chaitya. Square hall ringed by cells? Vihara.
  • Amalaka disc on top? Nagara. Storeyed pyramid plus gopuram? Dravida.
  • Ayaka pillars on the drum? An Andhra stupa — Amaravati or Nagarjunakonda.
  • Ganga and Yamuna on the doorjambs? A northern temple in the Gupta descent.
  • Stellate plan in soapstone? Hoysala, therefore Vesara.
  • Greek and Kharoshthi on one coin? Indo-Greek issue.
🔶 Bihar Connection
Bihar as the Workshop of Ancient Indian Art
  • Didarganj Yakshi (Patna): found in 1917 by the Ganga at Didarganj, a life-size female chauri-bearer in polished Chunar sandstone, now the centrepiece of the Bihar Museum, Patna. Its date is disputed — polish and stone argue for the Mauryan 3rd century BCE, while the drapery and ornament have led several scholars to a Kushana date. Answer "Mauryan" if forced, but know the dispute.
  • Barabar caves (Jehanabad): India's oldest surviving rock-cut caves, hollowed from granite and mirror-polished inside. Sudama and Karan Chaupar carry Ashoka's dedication to the Ajivikas; the Lomas Rishi facade turns a wooden barrel-vaulted hut into stone.
  • Nagarjuni caves (Jehanabad): Gopika, Vadathika and Vapiyaka, dedicated by Dasharatha Maurya, again to the Ajivikas. Pairing questions routinely separate the Ashokan group from the Dasharatha group.
  • Ashokan pillars of West Champaran: Lauriya Nandangarh stands complete with its lion capital in place, the best preserved of the series; Lauriya Areraj survives capital-less; Rampurva yielded two, its bull capital now at Rashtrapati Bhavan and its lion capital in the Indian Museum, Kolkata.
  • Kolhua pillar (Vaishali): a single-lion Ashokan pillar beside a brick stupa and the Ramkund tank — the pillar most often illustrated as "the Ashokan pillar of Bihar".
  • Mahabodhi temple, Bodh Gaya (Gaya): UNESCO World Heritage Site, 2002. The present structure is essentially Gupta, heavily restored in the nineteenth century, and its tower is odd — a tall straight-sided truncated pyramid, neither curvilinear Nagara nor storeyed Dravida vimana. The polished sandstone Vajrasana beside the Bodhi tree is attributed to Ashoka; parts of the railing are Shunga.
  • Nalanda (Nalanda district): UNESCO World Heritage Site, 2016. The textbook monastic layout — residential viharas on the east facing chaityas and stupas on the west across a central avenue. The Stupa of Sariputta (Site 3) keeps corner towers with Gupta-age stucco figures in niches.
  • Pala bronzes of Nalanda and Kurkihar: Nalanda produced a celebrated body of metal images, and the Kurkihar hoard (Gaya district) — over two hundred bronzes, largely in the Patna Museum — is the richest find of eastern Indian metalwork. Pala black-basalt sculpture, sharply finished and densely ornamented, closes ancient Indian sculpture.
  • Sultanganj Buddha (Bhagalpur): a standing copper Buddha over two metres tall, found in 1861 during railway work — the largest surviving metal Buddha from ancient India, now in the Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery.
  • Sonbhandar caves, Rajgir (Nalanda): two small rock-cut chambers on the Vaibhara hill, Jain in association, inscribed by the muni Vairadeva in Gupta-period script. They lack the Mauryan polish — do not confuse them with Barabar.
  • Kesaria stupa (East Champaran): a terraced brick stupa of enormous circumference with niched Buddhas, linked in tradition to the Buddha's parting gift of his alms bowl to the Licchavis.
  • Madhubani or Mithila painting (Madhubani, Darbhanga): Bihar's living folk tradition, painted by women on freshly plastered walls, above all in the kohbar ghar or nuptial chamber, and now on paper and cloth. Styles include Bharni, Katchni, Godna and Tantrik. It carries a Geographical Indication tag, and painters such as Sita Devi, Ganga Devi and Mahasundari Devi have won national honours.
🔗 Cross-Topic Connections
  • ↔ Topic 102 (Mauryan Empire): art is the physical proof of what the edicts assert. Chunar shafts hauled across the subcontinent, standardised capitals and polished granite caves show a resource mobilisation no text could establish; the Ajivika dedications show Ashoka's patronage ran wider than Buddhism.
  • ↔ Topic 103 (Post-Mauryan Period): imperial art becomes donative art. Sanchi's casing, the Bharhut railings and the Karle chaitya were funded by merchants, guilds and monks whose names are cut into the stones — evidence of the trading economy, and of the first Buddha images made under Kushana patronage.
  • ↔ Topic 104 (Gupta Empire): the Gupta age supplies the first structural temples, the Sarnath Buddha type, the Dhamek stupa, the Ajanta paintings under their Vakataka allies, Nalanda's founding and the classical Sanskrit corpus; the gold dinaras are art objects and economic evidence at once.
  • ↔ Topic 137 (Early Medieval Period): the regional temple styles branch here — Odisha, Khajuraho and Solanki within Nagara, the Chola elaboration of Dravida, the Vesara experiments of the Deccan — while the Palas make eastern India the last centre of Buddhist art before it migrates to Tibet and Southeast Asia.
  • ↔ Topic 155 (Ancient Bihar): almost every strand here has a Bihar anchor — Barabar and Nagarjuni, the Champaran and Vaishali pillars, the Didarganj Yakshi, Bodh Gaya, Nalanda, Kurkihar, Sultanganj. Revise the two chapters together and the state-specific questions answer themselves.
  • ↔ Art as social evidence: donative inscriptions for guilds and women's property, Mrichchhakatika for urban life, Sangam poetry for Tamil polity, the epics for the values of the age that compiled them.
